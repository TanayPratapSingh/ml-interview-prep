# Stanford CS229 Machine Learning, Spring 2026

CS229 teaches the foundations of machine learning with an emphasis on derivations: linear and logistic regression, generalized linear models, generative classifiers, bias, variance and regularization, neural networks with backpropagation, and unsupervised learning (K-means, Gaussian mixtures, EM and PCA), before moving to diffusion models, language models and reinforcement learning. Lectures below are numbered by playlist order, so a few YouTube titles differ, and every timestamp link opens the video at the moment where the idea is taught.

## Lecture 1: Course overview and the three kinds of learning

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8)

This first lecture is a relaxed map of the course and of machine learning as a field. It covers the logistics (prerequisites, the mathematical focus, the AI tool policy), two classic definitions of machine learning, and a tour of supervised learning, unsupervised learning and reinforcement learning, with examples that run from house prices and ImageNet to word vectors, large language models and diffusion models. The lecturer stresses that the three paradigms now overlap, and that reinforcement learning has become a tool for training language models because sampled text cannot be differentiated. Nothing is derived here; the lecture shows what the later lectures will teach.

### Key ideas

- [1:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=68s) The course expects probability and linear algebra. Anyone missing pieces can use the Friday sections to catch up, but a student who knows nothing about them is advised to take the course later.
- [2:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=131s) The course is mathematically intensive: derivations and the formulation of mathematical models matter more than programming. The goal is to understand the basics of algorithms, not to prove theorems.
- [2:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=163s) AI tools are allowed as colleagues, but you may not paste generated code or results into your answers. The reasoning is that the course trains you, not an agent.
- [4:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=259s) The traditional approach to machine learning is still relevant. The methodology for training models is close to what it was; what changed is how models are used (a query to a pretrained model instead of a hand built pipeline).
- [5:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=324s) Two classic definitions: the 1959 one (learning without being explicitly programmed) and Tom Mitchell's one in terms of experience E, tasks T and performance P. A program learns if its performance on T, measured by P, improves with E.
- [6:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=388s) Experience now includes synthetic data, model generated thinking tokens, internet data and human labels. Tasks have become universal, since one model can be asked to solve a million problems. The performance measure guides training, and if more data does not help, nothing is being learned.
- [8:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=484s) One reasonable taxonomy is supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning, but there is no consensus and the three overlap. Today they are seen more as methods than as goals; supervised fine tuning, for example, is a step in building general agents.
- [9:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=579s) Supervised learning, shown through house prices: each example pairs an input x (square footage) with an output y (price), and you fit a line or a quadratic to predict y for a new x. Lectures 2 and 3 cover fitting such functions, loss functions and optimizers.
- [11:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=709s) Terminology: inputs or features are what you are given, outputs or labels are what you predict, and people say annotation for supplying the label (by a human or a machine). Adding plot size as a second input makes the problem two dimensional in input.
- [12:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=741s) Regression predicts a continuous output such as price. Classification predicts a discrete label such as single family home versus townhouse, and can be pictured as a boundary that splits the input space.
- [13:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=838s) Language models are classifiers: predicting the next token means choosing one label out of tens of thousands (roughly 50 thousand to 250 thousand tokens).
- [15:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=901s) ImageNet, built by a Stanford professor and her team, has about one million image and label pairs, roughly 20 times larger than the second biggest dataset of its time. AlexNet, trained on it by Hinton, Krizhevsky and Sutskever, started the current era of AI.
- [17:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1029s) Other supervised tasks: object localization, where y is four numbers (two corners of a bounding box), and machine translation, where x and y are sentences in two languages. The course teaches the fundamental techniques, not the applications.
- [18:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1092s) Deep learning is the technology behind these results: neural networks with billions of parameters, trained by computing gradients of a loss with backpropagation. Architectures and backpropagation get their own lectures.
- [19:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1192s) Unsupervised learning has inputs only and no labels, and the goal of finding interesting structure is vague because different methods find different structure.
- [21:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1287s) Clustering examples: grouping individuals by gene expression patterns and grouping documents by word counts, where reordering a word by document table exposes topics such as space launches versus air pollution. The word count technique is called LSA.
- [24:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1447s) Word embeddings map each word to a vector (sometimes thousands of numbers). Similar words get nearby vectors, and a direction can encode a relation, such as country to capital. Early embeddings were trained on unlabeled text like Wikipedia.
- [25:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1542s) Large language models are general purpose models trained on massive unlabeled data, in contrast to older methods that served one or two tasks. The course covers the architecture, the pre training loss, prompting and how post training makes models follow instructions.
- [27:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1669s) Diffusion models generate realistic images from noise or from instructions, trained on many unlabeled images. There is one lecture on them.
- [28:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1703s) Reinforcement learning in its traditional form is about sequential decisions: AlphaGo and robot locomotion are examples, and the actions taken now affect what can happen later.
- [30:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1800s) RL is also used to train language models. Generation is stochastic sampling, which is not differentiable, so backpropagation cannot flow through it, and each generated token can be treated as a decision that affects the next.
- [31:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1898s) RL also changes where data comes from: the agent alternates between collecting data (trajectories of actions and observations) and training, so it learns from data it generated itself.
- [33:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=1993s) Automatic theorem proving as a self reinforcing loop: generate candidate proofs, keep the correct ones, and train on them so the next round produces more correct proofs. Robotics does the same with high quality trajectories.
- [33:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=2025s) For language models a reward module (a human, a reward model or another LLM) scores generated text and the score updates the model. The method is called policy gradient, and RLHF and long chain of thought training are applications.
- [35:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DATnpGoGhM8&t=2122s) The syllabus also includes a lecture on machine learning systems (matching software to hardware, because a 20 percent speedup is worth a great deal at current costs) and a planned guest talk on the social impact of AI.

## Lecture 2: Linear regression, gradient descent and SGD

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM)

This lecture sets up supervised learning formally and builds linear regression as the first complete example. It defines a hypothesis as a function from inputs to outputs, introduces empirical risk minimization and the least squares cost, and then derives gradient descent, minibatch stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and the normal equations. The lecturer argues that machine learning cares about predictions on new data rather than recovering true parameters, and spends a long stretch on how step size and batch size are chosen in practice. The notation (vectors, matrices, invertibility, positive semidefiniteness) is used freely, so students are told to review it in the Friday sections.

### Key ideas

- [5:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=349s) A hypothesis is a function from an input set X to an output set Y. X can be images, text or house data, and Y can be a fixed set of labels or a number such as a price.
- [6:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=413s) What makes the setting supervised is the training set: collected pairs of x and y, with a superscript in parentheses indexing the examples.
- [8:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=507s) A good hypothesis is one that generalizes, meaning it works on inputs outside the training set. For simple hypothesis classes this can be proved, and modern machine learning uses very large classes with enormous numbers of parameters.
- [10:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=601s) Terminology: continuous y is regression and discrete y is classification. Regression is taught first because the math is simpler, though classification dominates practice (a language model has a classifier on its output that guesses the next word).
- [11:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=663s) The worked dataset is the Ames housing data. The advice is to always look at your data first, even when building a complex system.
- [12:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=726s) A linear hypothesis, technically affine, is h(x) = theta_1 x + theta_0, which restricts the huge space of functions to lines. Linear models are the workhorse and the last step of many models you use.
- [13:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=821s) Such a class is parametric: it is fully described by two numbers whatever the number of houses. Nearest neighbors, which averages the neighbors of x, is the contrasting idea.
- [15:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=917s) Choosing the line that minimizes prediction errors on the training set is empirical risk minimization (ERM).
- [16:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1012s) No line fits perfectly because prices depend on things the model does not see and because some error is always left over. This motivates studying training error versus test error later.
- [17:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1075s) Adding the number of bedrooms makes x a vector and the hypothesis class richer: setting weights to zero recovers the smaller model.
- [18:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1139s) With the convention x_0 = 1, h_theta(x) is simply a sum of theta_j x_j over all components, so the intercept needs no special case and the formula extends to any number of features.
- [20:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1236s) Which features to feed in is a modeling decision. In classical practice you write code to turn raw data into numbers; deep learning later learns the features.
- [21:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1304s) Notation: n is the number of examples, d the number of features, and with the convention the vectors have d + 1 entries.
- [23:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1398s) Modern models have millions to trillions of parameters and work in spaces with thousands of dimensions, so vector notation is how we reason about them.
- [24:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1460s) Least squares: pick theta to minimize J(theta), half the sum over examples of the squared difference between h_theta(x) and y. The arg min notation returns the minimizing theta.
- [25:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1555s) Why squares? Absolute value is a valid alternative, but squared error gives a clean gradient and an exact solution, which is why Gauss used it. A normal distribution of errors justifies it statistically, a topic for a later lecture.
- [28:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1681s) The one half is a convention. The arg min does not change if the objective is scaled, and the half cancels the 2 from differentiating.
- [28:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1712s) The parameters are continuous, so this is continuous optimization. Even models of discrete data such as words compute with continuous parameters under the hood, which makes gradients available.
- [29:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1774s) With lots of data the exact form of the loss matters less than people expect. The softmax loss used for classification comes next lecture.
- [31:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=1869s) The core paradigm: a good hypothesis is close to the training data, and we trust that this transfers to the real world. This is a statistical leap of faith, and the lecturer promises examples where it failed.
- [34:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2060s) Gradient descent: near theta_0 the function is approximated by its first order Taylor expansion, so stepping against the gradient lowers it. Only local slope information is used.
- [35:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2158s) Procedure: start from an initial guess (random or zero, which does not matter for least squares but does in general), compute the gradient, and step in the opposite direction by an amount called the step size or learning rate.
- [37:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2220s) Because only local information is used, a non convex function can trap the iterate in a local minimum. Convex, bowl shaped functions have none.
- [37:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2253s) The update rule for least squares: each coordinate moves by the step size times the sum over examples of the error times the matching feature. The error times input pattern recurs in later models.
- [38:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2315s) Scanning the whole dataset for every step is hopeless at internet scale, which motivates cheaper algorithms.
- [39:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2378s) The step size expresses how much you trust the gradient. Too small and progress is slow, too large and the iterates overshoot and bounce across the bowl.
- [40:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2442s) SGD bounces inside a ball whose size is proportional to the step. Statistics wants the correct theta, but machine learning only needs to be close enough, and runs are often stopped when the compute budget ends.
- [42:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2539s) Machine learning is oriented toward more data and bigger models rather than ten digit accuracy in the parameters, which leads to scaling laws later.
- [43:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2634s) Minibatch SGD, going back to Robbins and Monro in the 1950s, computes the gradient on a small random batch instead of the whole dataset. It is the workhorse behind PyTorch style training.
- [45:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2728s) Batches must be random samples. Showing all cats and then all dogs would teach the model one extreme and then the other. This is the second statistical assumption: batches represent the training set.
- [47:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2829s) Sampling with or without replacement hardly matters in theory and practice; shuffling once and sweeping is simpler to implement.
- [48:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2923s) Coupon collector intuition: with random draws, the last rare item takes disproportionately long to appear, so shuffling avoids missing rare data.
- [49:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=2955s) Batch size is set mostly by hardware in practice. The lecturer recounts that the old belief in small batches was overturned by results where training loss and generalization disagreed.
- [51:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3079s) Optimization is a leaky abstraction: lower training loss does not always mean better test performance. Machine learning is not statistics.
- [52:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3142s) Small batches were once preferred because with redundant data (90 photos of the same cat) a full batch wastes steps.
- [53:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3236s) Batches can also be designed, for example mixing positive and negative examples that look similar, a theme of the second half of the course.
- [54:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3299s) Modern practice chooses the batch size to fit the fast memory on the GPU (HBM) and keep the hardware busy.
- [56:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3394s) The minibatch update sums errors over the batch and uses a step size alpha_B that may differ from the full batch one.
- [57:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3426s) Adaptive optimizers such as Adam, AdamW and Adagrad pick step sizes for you.
- [59:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3553s) Step size schedules: a cosine schedule worked well for image models, and several other decay rules turn out to be closely related.
- [61:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3678s) In language model training some data (such as code) is deliberately repeated, a topic called data mixing.
- [63:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3805s) At large scale the priority is keeping GPUs busy: labs report MFU (model flops utilization), and statistical subtleties are often secondary.
- [64:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3867s) Surprisingly, a lot of settings work even when the theory says they should not matter much, such as swapping the classification loss.
- [65:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=3932s) Full batch gradient descent on a smooth bowl goes straight to the optimum. SGD zigzags, and a too large step makes it bounce chaotically. Averaging the iterates imitates a larger batch.
- [67:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=4027s) In the second half of the course the functions will not be convex bowls, and smoothness arguments stop helping.
- [68:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=4122s) There is no principled stopping rule for large neural networks. You watch the loss, and abilities can switch on suddenly, so you cannot tell whether you are stuck or close.
- [69:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=4184s) For convex models (generalized linear models, next lectures) you can tell how far from the optimum you are.
- [70:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=4248s) The normal equations: stack the inputs into an n by (d + 1) design matrix X and the labels into y, so that J is half the squared norm of X theta minus y.
- [74:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=4443s) Setting the gradient to zero gives X transpose X theta equal to X transpose y, so theta equals the inverse of X transpose X times X transpose y.
- [75:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=4511s) This assumes X transpose X is invertible. If it is not, the null space gives a whole subspace of equally good solutions. X transpose X is always positive semidefinite.
- [77:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmNIMjPYdgM&t=4639s) Recap: linear regression is one of many models in the course, and stochastic gradient descent is the main tool that returns again and again. Next lecture moves to classification.

### Formulas

Linear hypothesis with the convention that the first feature is the constant 1 (here the vectors have d + 1 entries):

$$
h_\theta(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\theta_j x_j=\theta^\top x,\qquad x_0=1
$$

Least squares cost and its minimizer:

$$
J(\theta)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(h_\theta(x^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)^2,\qquad
\hat\theta=\arg\min_\theta J(\theta)
$$

First order approximation behind gradient descent:

$$
f(\theta)\approx f(\theta_0)+f'(\theta_0)\,(\theta-\theta_0)
$$

Batch gradient descent update for least squares (step size alpha):

$$
\theta_j:=\theta_j-\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(h_\theta(x^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)\,x_j^{(i)}
$$

Minibatch SGD update, with a random batch B and step size alpha_B:

$$
\theta:=\theta-\alpha_B\sum_{i\in B}\big(h_\theta(x^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\big)\,x^{(i)}
$$

Normal equations in matrix form, for the design matrix X and label vector y:

$$
J(\theta)=\tfrac12\|X\theta-y\|_2^2,\qquad
\nabla_\theta J=X^\top X\theta-X^\top y=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat\theta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y
$$

## Lecture 3: Maximum likelihood, logistic regression and Newton's method

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI). The YouTube title says Weighted Least Squares, but the transcript never covers that topic

This lecture gives least squares a probabilistic justification and then uses the same idea to build logistic regression for classification. Assuming the labels are a linear signal plus independent Gaussian noise, maximizing the likelihood of the data turns out to be exactly the least squares problem. The lecturer calls this maximum likelihood recipe the most important idea to take away: write a probability model, take logs to get a sum, and optimize with gradient methods. The lecture ends with Newton's method, which takes far fewer steps than gradient descent but costs far more per step, which is why minibatch SGD remains the workhorse.

### Key ideas

- [1:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=68s) Logistic regression, or its multi class cousin softmax, is the last layer of nearly every chatbot or text and code model you have used.
- [2:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=131s) The main takeaway of the lecture is maximum likelihood: write down a probabilistic model of how the world behaves, and that determines which parameters you search for. It generalizes from continuous to discrete outputs and up to modern AI models.
- [5:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=321s) The model: each label is generated from a hidden true theta plus a noise term epsilon. We assume this process and ask which theta makes the data most believable.
- [6:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=384s) Noise can be real measurement error or things you choose not to model. Modeling is abstraction, and the useful question is whether a model is useful, not whether it is correct.
- [7:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=478s) Assumption one: the noise is random with zero mean, so it carries no systematic bias that would hide a shift from the regression.
- [9:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=540s) Assumption two: the noise values are independent and identically distributed (iid), a strong assumption chosen partly for computational convenience.
- [9:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=571s) iid can fail, for example when data from two sources has different error variances. That can be modeled, but this basic model ignores it.
- [10:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=635s) Only the first nontrivial property of the noise, its variance, is modeled.
- [11:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=667s) These assumptions lead to the Gaussian distribution, a good first approximation justified by the law of large numbers and central limit theorems. What matters is whether it is good enough for your purpose.
- [13:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=828s) Notation: Z distributed as a normal with mean mu and variance sigma squared, written with a tilde, and a conditional model written with a bar and a semicolon.
- [15:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=925s) The Gaussian density has a normalizing constant (the least important part) and an exponent containing the squared distance from the mean divided by two sigma squared, which is where least squares will come from.
- [17:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=1021s) About 99.7 percent of the probability lies within three sigma. A small sigma gives a sharp peak and a large sigma gives a flat one.
- [17:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=1052s) A single Gaussian is symmetric and has one peak. Skewed or multimodal data needs more, such as the mixture of Gaussians model covered in a later lecture.
- [20:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=1211s) Given x and theta, the label y is Gaussian with mean theta transpose x and the same variance sigma squared as the noise.
- [22:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=1336s) The distribution is conditioned on the observed inputs x and parameterized by theta. Theta is not an event we condition on.
- [22:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=1367s) Maximum likelihood: among all distributions that could have generated the dataset, choose the theta that makes the data most likely. We search over theta, not over the data.
- [25:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=1524s) Independence lets the joint probability of all examples factor into a product of one term per example, which turns a huge function into n small ones.
- [27:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=1620s) Taking logarithms turns the product into a sum, which is numerically stable and easy to differentiate. The log is monotone, so the best theta does not change.
- [28:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=1687s) The log likelihood is a constant minus the least squares cost J, so maximizing it is the same as minimizing J. Least squares is maximum likelihood with iid Gaussian noise of fixed variance.
- [31:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=1874s) Maximum likelihood is more general than least squares: it is easier to model a new problem by choosing a probabilistic model, and the same optimization recipe follows.
- [33:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2000s) The noise variance can be assumed, fitted separately, or fitted jointly. If a small change in such a setting changes your conclusions a lot, be worried.
- [34:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2095s) Binary classification has labels in 0 or 1. Why not just apply least squares?
- [35:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2157s) Least squares tries to fit a surface through the label values, so far away points pull the fit and shift the boundary, even though only the separating gap matters.
- [37:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2221s) That said, least squares often works better than expected on real data, since modeling is a matter of degree. High dimensional data behaves strangely.
- [37:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2253s) Outputs such as 7 or negative 40 do not make sense for a yes or no label, so a classification model should output a probability.
- [38:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2285s) Logistic regression keeps the linear part and passes it through a link function g that is smooth and monotone. The popular choice is the sigmoid.
- [39:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2348s) A hard threshold is non differentiable, which makes it mathematically inconvenient, though non differentiable functions such as ReLU are used elsewhere.
- [39:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2380s) The output h_theta(x) is read as the probability that the label is 1, and one minus it as the probability of the other class.
- [41:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2506s) The label probability can be written compactly as h to the y times one minus h to the one minus y. The product over examples is then logged to get a sum.
- [43:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2600s) The log likelihood is an additive loss, so you differentiate and run gradient or stochastic gradient methods. Maximizing means ascent, with the sign flipped relative to descent.
- [44:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2694s) The recipe: model with maximum likelihood, convert to a convenient additive form, solve with gradient methods.
- [45:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2726s) The gradient of the logistic log likelihood again has the form error times feature, a pattern shared by a large family of models.
- [47:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2854s) Gradient descent is a first order method: it uses the local error to pick a step direction.
- [48:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2886s) Historically logistic regression was a basic working tool, often with huge numbers of random features, and a convex problem so you could find the exact theta.
- [49:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2949s) Machine learning people eventually stopped caring about getting the same theta each time and cared about the predictions h_theta instead.
- [49:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=2980s) Newton's method finds a zero of a function. Applied to the gradient of the log likelihood, it finds a stationary point.
- [50:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=3051s) It uses the local linear model (Taylor approximation) and jumps to where the line crosses zero. There is no step size.
- [53:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=3210s) In higher dimensions the update uses the inverse of the Hessian, or a pseudoinverse when the Hessian is singular.
- [55:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=3336s) Per step cost: SGD is order d, minibatch is batch size times d, full gradient descent is n times d, and Newton is n times d squared plus d cubed.
- [57:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=3435s) Newton's method needs the fewest steps but each is very expensive, so minibatch SGD is the workhorse at scale.
- [57:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=3465s) Classical statistics still uses Newton or L-BFGS, a limited memory second order method, for models with a few hundred features or fewer, where parameter accuracy matters.
- [59:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=3590s) Adagrad and AdaDelta estimate curvature cheaply (for example only its diagonal), and Adam can be seen as combining those ideas with momentum.
- [61:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uJF_gL3jhxI&t=3686s) Recap: classification versus regression, the maximum likelihood principle, logistic regression and Newton's method. Next lecture extends this to generalized linear models.

### Formulas

Linear regression as a probabilistic model, with iid Gaussian noise:

$$
y^{(i)}=\theta^\top x^{(i)}+\epsilon^{(i)},\qquad \epsilon^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)
\;\Longrightarrow\;
y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)};\theta\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}(\theta^\top x^{(i)},\sigma^2)
$$

Gaussian density:

$$
p(z;\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)
$$

Likelihood under independence, and the log likelihood:

$$
L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p\big(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)};\theta\big),\qquad
\ell(\theta)=\log L(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p\big(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)};\theta\big)
$$

Gaussian noise gives least squares:

$$
\ell(\theta)=n\log\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\cdot\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y^{(i)}-\theta^\top x^{(i)}\big)^2
=\text{const}-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}J(\theta)
$$

Logistic regression hypothesis with the sigmoid:

$$
h_\theta(x)=g(\theta^\top x),\qquad g(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},\qquad
P(y=1\mid x;\theta)=h_\theta(x)
$$

Logistic regression likelihood and log likelihood:

$$
p(y\mid x;\theta)=h_\theta(x)^{y}\big(1-h_\theta(x)\big)^{1-y},\qquad
\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y^{(i)}\log h_\theta(x^{(i)})+\big(1-y^{(i)}\big)\log\big(1-h_\theta(x^{(i)})\big)\Big]
$$

Gradient ascent for logistic regression:

$$
\theta_j:=\theta_j+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y^{(i)}-h_\theta(x^{(i)})\big)\,x_j^{(i)}
$$

Newton's method for a root, and for maximizing the log likelihood (H is the Hessian):

$$
\theta:=\theta-\frac{f(\theta)}{f'(\theta)},\qquad
\theta:=\theta-H^{-1}\nabla_\theta\ell(\theta)
$$

## Lecture 4: Exponential family, GLMs, softmax and cross entropy

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg)

This lecture shows that least squares and logistic regression are two instances of one construction. A large class of distributions called the exponential family can be written in a single form, and for any member you get inference, maximum likelihood training and an SGD update with the same error times feature shape. Choosing the distribution to match the type of label (binary, categorical, real, counts) and making its natural parameter linear in the input gives a generalized linear model. The second half builds softmax regression for many classes, shows it reduces to logistic regression with two classes, and derives cross entropy and label smoothing, the loss that sits at the end of modern language models.

### Key ideas

- [0:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=36s) The exponential family is a cornerstone of statistics: whatever the data type (real values, classes, counts), it gives one reliable path from a distribution over the labels to a trained model, using the same procedure.
- [1:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=99s) The payoff is a family of generalized linear models (GLMs), with inference and training given almost for free and solved by SGD. The most important member today is softmax, used to choose the next token among all words in a vocabulary.
- [3:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=195s) A distribution is in the exponential family if it can be written as b(y) times exp of (eta transpose T(y) minus a(eta)). If it has this shape, inference and training come with it.
- [4:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=258s) T(y) is the sufficient statistic (in this course simply y), b(y) is the base measure and does not depend on eta, and a(eta) is the log partition function.
- [5:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=321s) The log partition function is a normalizer: it sums or integrates over all possible values of y so that the distribution adds up to one. Most of the mathematical action lives in it.
- [7:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=448s) Example one, a coin toss: the Bernoulli distribution with parameter phi, written as phi to the y times one minus phi to the one minus y.
- [9:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=549s) Taking logs and grouping the terms that multiply y reveals the log odds, log of phi over one minus phi, as the natural parameter eta.
- [10:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=645s) Inverting eta gives phi as one over one plus e to the minus eta, which is the sigmoid from logistic regression. This is a sanity check that Bernoulli fits the form.
- [12:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=774s) Example two, the Gaussian with fixed variance: after algebra the terms with y squared go into b(y), the term linear in y pairs with eta, and the rest becomes a(eta), with eta equal to the mean.
- [14:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=841s) The form can have complicated functions of y and of eta, but the interaction between them must be linear. That is what makes the models linear in the natural parameter.
- [15:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=936s) The derivative of the log partition function a(eta) is the expectation of T(y). The Gaussian result (the mean) is not a coincidence but holds for the whole family, because differentiating the log of a sum gives a weighted average.
- [17:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1033s) The second derivative of a(eta) gives the variance (or covariance). Higher cumulants are different from higher moments, though the first few coincide.
- [19:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1190s) Since the second derivative is a covariance matrix, which is positive semidefinite, a is convex. So training has no spurious local minima, parameters can be recovered, and gradient descent works.
- [20:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1253s) Members of the family include Bernoulli (binary data), multinomial (several classes, which leads to softmax), Gaussian (real values), Poisson (counts), gamma and exponential (positive values), and Dirichlet (distributions over distributions).
- [22:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1348s) Thinking in terms of errors means choosing the error distribution that matches your label type. Once you have chosen it, the rest of the model follows.
- [24:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1442s) In practice you use a framework that implements the softmax and the gradients, so this is mainly a tool for understanding. Understanding the components helps diagnose bugs, such as a wrong weight initialization in a reimplemented model.
- [28:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1696s) A generalized linear model: choose a distribution for y given x according to the type of y (Bernoulli for binary, multinomial for several classes).
- [29:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1759s) The model is made linear by setting the natural parameter eta equal to theta transpose x. Theta holds the weights we learn.
- [30:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1821s) The prediction is the expectation of y given x: h_theta(x) equals the mean of the distribution. For logistic regression this is the probability of class 1.
- [30:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1853s) Training is maximum likelihood. With the iid assumption the likelihood is a product, so the log likelihood is a sum over examples, and we run gradient ascent.
- [32:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=1920s) For every GLM the SGD update has the same form: error (prediction minus label) times the features. Linear regression and logistic regression are special cases.
- [33:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2013s) Terminology: theta are the model parameters, theta transpose x is the natural parameter eta, and a response (link) function turns it into the canonical parameters such as the Bernoulli probabilities. Only theta is learned.
- [35:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2143s) The iid assumption enters where the log of the joint probability is written as a sum, and identical distribution means we do not mix different distributions across examples.
- [37:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2237s) Multi class classification is what a language model does for every generated token: choose one of K discrete values.
- [37:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2269s) Classes are encoded as one hot vectors, such as cat, dog, car and bus as e1 through e4. A prediction is a distribution over classes on the simplex, for instance 0.5, 0.3, 0.1, 0.1.
- [40:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2400s) Softmax: each class k has its own parameter vector theta_k, the score is theta_k dot x, and the probability is the exponential of the score divided by the sum of exponentials over all classes.
- [41:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2495s) Picture: each class has a hyperplane where its score is zero, with theta_k as the normal vector. The score grows in the direction of theta_k, and the class with the highest score wins where regions conflict.
- [45:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2751s) In low dimensions many datasets cannot be separated by lines, but in high dimensions (768 for GPT-2 in the lecturer's example) things separate much more easily. Kernels, covered later, push this to infinite dimensions.
- [46:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2818s) With K classes this generalizes logistic regression, because each class is weighed against all the alternatives.
- [47:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2850s) The exponential suppresses classes that are far away: a score difference of 5 gives a factor of e to the minus 5, so clearly separated points get nearly clean probabilities while ambiguous points keep non zero probability.
- [49:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=2945s) A distinction between inference and generation: you could pick only the most likely token, but in practice the full distribution is sampled, so different runs give different outputs. The lecturer's student paper on sampling many times from a model is called Large Language Monkeys.
- [52:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=3138s) An image with both a cat and a dog does not fit this model because the classes are mutually exclusive. Object detection or multi label models are a different error model.
- [53:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=3234s) Worked example: raw scores are exponentiated and normalized so that the probabilities sum to one.
- [58:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=3503s) With K = 2, the softmax reduces exactly to logistic regression: only K minus 1 parameter vectors are free, and the difference theta_1 minus theta_2 plays the role of theta.
- [60:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=3630s) Training maximizes the log probability of the correct class. This is the cross entropy loss. With a one hot label only the correct class term survives.
- [63:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=3822s) Label smoothing replaces the one hot target by a distribution that puts most mass on the true class and a little on others (for example 1 minus 2 epsilon on the true class and epsilon on two neighbors).
- [64:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=3864s) Why smooth: labels can be wrong, training on one example for long would make the model overconfident, and it works as regularization.
- [66:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=3992s) If you know which classes are plausible confusions (a cat or a dog, but not a car), that information can go into the target distribution used in the cross entropy.
- [68:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=4085s) Why softmax? It gives smooth interpolation between 0 and 1, it is natural and numerically convenient, and it passes gradient to all classes. It has a maximum entropy justification but is not the unique choice.
- [72:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8gVi4Rk21Eg&t=4340s) Recap: exponential families generalize everything learned so far. Softmax with cross entropy underlies the final layer of the modern AI systems built later in the course.

### Formulas

Exponential family (eta is the natural parameter, T the sufficient statistic, b the base measure, a the log partition function):

$$
p(y;\eta)=b(y)\,\exp\!\big(\eta^\top T(y)-a(\eta)\big)
$$

Bernoulli as a member, with T(y) = y:

$$
\phi^{y}(1-\phi)^{1-y}=\exp\!\Big(y\log\frac{\phi}{1-\phi}+\log(1-\phi)\Big),\qquad
\eta=\log\frac{\phi}{1-\phi},\quad \phi=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta}},\quad a(\eta)=\log\big(1+e^{\eta}\big)
$$

Gaussian with fixed variance 1, with eta equal to the mean mu:

$$
p(y;\mu)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^2/2}\exp\!\Big(\mu y-\frac{\mu^2}{2}\Big),\qquad a(\eta)=\frac{\eta^2}{2}
$$

The log partition function gives the mean and the variance:

$$
a(\eta)=\log\!\int b(y)\,e^{\eta^\top T(y)}\,dy,\qquad
\nabla_\eta a(\eta)=\mathbb{E}[T(y);\eta],\qquad \nabla^2_\eta a(\eta)=\operatorname{Cov}\big(T(y);\eta\big)\succeq 0
$$

Generalized linear model: natural parameter linear in x, prediction is the mean, and the SGD update for T(y) = y:

$$
\eta=\theta^\top x,\qquad h_\theta(x)=\mathbb{E}[y\mid x;\theta],\qquad
\theta:=\theta+\alpha\big(y^{(i)}-h_\theta(x^{(i)})\big)x^{(i)}
$$

Softmax regression over K classes:

$$
p(y=k\mid x;\theta)=\frac{\exp(\theta_k^\top x)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(\theta_j^\top x)}
$$

Cross entropy loss for a target distribution p hat (one hot gives minus log of the true class probability), and its gradient with respect to the scores z:

$$
\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat p_k\log p_k,\qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_k}=p_k-\hat p_k
$$

Two class softmax reduces to the sigmoid of the difference:

$$
\frac{e^{\theta_1^\top x}}{e^{\theta_1^\top x}+e^{\theta_2^\top x}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\theta_1-\theta_2)^\top x}}
$$

## Lecture 5: Generative models: GDA and naive Bayes

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 5](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes)

This lecture switches from discriminative models, which learn p(y given x), to generative models, which learn p(x given y) and the class prior and classify with Bayes' rule. It builds Gaussian discriminant analysis (GDA), including the multivariate Gaussian and its covariance matrix, derives closed form maximum likelihood estimates, and shows that with a shared covariance the decision boundary is linear and the posterior has the logistic form. The lecturer then compares GDA with logistic regression and argues that the classical trade off story undersells how much progress has come from generative modeling of unlabeled data (GPT, diffusion, GANs). The lecture ends with naive Bayes for discrete features such as spam filtering, with its conditional independence assumption and Laplace smoothing.

### Key ideas

- [0:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=5s) Generative models are the G in GPT. GDA is the first and simplest generative algorithm, in which you learn a representation of the data and then use it to classify.
- [3:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=226s) Everything so far (linear regression, logistic regression, GLMs) was discriminative: it models p(y given x). A generative model instead models p(x given y) together with the class prior.
- [5:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=353s) Cats versus elephants by height and weight: a discriminative model learns only the boundary between the classes, while a generative model models what each class looks like on its own.
- [8:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=514s) To classify a new point, ask how probable it is under the cat model and under the elephant model, weight by the class priors, and apply Bayes' rule.
- [12:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=735s) The multivariate Gaussian is written with a mean vector mu and a covariance matrix Sigma, which is symmetric and positive definite.
- [13:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=799s) Positive definite means z transpose Sigma z is positive for every nonzero z. Equivalently all eigenvalues are positive, so Sigma is invertible and its determinant is positive.
- [16:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=988s) With Sigma equal to the identity the contours are circles, and each coordinate is independent. A diagonal Sigma stretches each axis separately and gives axis aligned ellipses.
- [18:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=1113s) A full Sigma both stretches and rotates the contours, and its off diagonal entries encode positive or negative correlation. Changing mu only shifts the center.
- [21:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=1303s) Zeros in the inverse covariance matrix mean two variables are conditionally independent. This is the basis of graphical models and is not required for the course.
- [24:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=1465s) The GDA model: y is Bernoulli with parameter phi, x given y = 0 is Gaussian with mean mu_0, and x given y = 1 is Gaussian with mean mu_1, with the same covariance Sigma for both classes.
- [26:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=1563s) Sharing the covariance means both classes have contours of identical shape and orientation. Only their centers differ.
- [27:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=1659s) A full covariance has on the order of d squared parameters, which is expensive in high dimensions, so sharing one covariance across classes is a modeling choice.
- [28:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=1723s) The class prior matters: with a million cats and one elephant, a point between the two sizes is far more likely a cat. Bayes' rule weighs the evidence and the prior together.
- [29:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=1786s) GDA assumes the class proportions seen in training match those at test time, and that the training data is representative.
- [32:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=1948s) Four things are learned: the class prior phi, the two class means mu_0 and mu_1, and the shared covariance Sigma. This is more structure than the d + 1 weights of logistic regression.
- [34:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=2074s) Given iid data, write the joint likelihood of the examples and labels as a product of p(x given y) times p(y), then take logs.
- [36:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=2170s) Modeling the joint p(x, y) is world modeling: you model how likely the data and the labels are together. Energy based models and diffusion also model data directly.
- [36:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=2201s) You could use gradient descent, but for GDA you can set the derivatives to zero and solve in closed form.
- [37:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=2263s) The maximum likelihood estimates: phi is the fraction of examples in class 1, each class mean is the average of the points in that class, and Sigma pools the residuals around each point's own class mean.
- [40:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=2457s) A closed form solution is still learning, since parameters are estimated from data. It just avoids the iterative procedure.
- [42:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=2552s) The derivation uses that Sigma is invertible. If it is not, the null space leaves some directions of the mean unidentified.
- [45:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=2746s) Prediction is arg max over y of p(x given y) p(y). The denominator p(x) does not depend on y, so it can be dropped.
- [47:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=2840s) Setting the two class scores equal and taking logs, the quadratic terms in x cancel because Sigma is shared, leaving a linear function of x.
- [48:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=2904s) So the GDA decision boundary is a hyperplane, and the log odds is linear in x, even though the modeling was probabilistic.
- [56:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=3382s) With a separate covariance for each class the quadratic terms no longer cancel and the boundary is curved. This is quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA).
- [57:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=3445s) Far from the data, a boundary can behave oddly, because a linear (or quadratic) model is being used where it never saw examples.
- [58:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=3507s) Using Bayes' rule, the GDA posterior p(y = 1 given x) has exactly the logistic regression form, a sigmoid of a linear function of x.
- [60:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=3601s) Many other exponential family generative assumptions also lead to a logistic posterior. This is no accident: such models are log linear in their parameters.
- [62:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=3759s) Progress in recent years came from generative models of unlabeled data. GPT is pretrained to predict the next token on huge unlabeled text, in regimes with far more parameters than data points that statistics once avoided.
- [63:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=3823s) The reverse does not hold: logistic regression does not imply GDA. GDA makes stronger assumptions, while logistic regression only assumes the log odds is linear.
- [64:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=3885s) The textbook trade off: GDA is efficient if its assumptions hold and fragile if not, and logistic regression needs more data but is safer. The lecturer calls this somewhat misleading.
- [65:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=3916s) Diffusion models add Gaussian noise step by step to an image and learn to reverse it, so random noise can be turned into a new image. LLMs and GANs also have generative parts.
- [71:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=4296s) Early BERT style models were pretrained generatively, then their embeddings were used with a discriminative head for classification. This leads to adapters and fine tuning later.
- [73:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=4392s) Unlike GLMs, there is no single unified family that covers all generative models, since they need different structures.
- [74:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=4455s) Spam filtering and sentiment analysis are classic classification tasks, and the simplest generative approach is naive Bayes with Bayes' rule.
- [74:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=4487s) Build a dictionary of informative words (leaving out stop words) and represent each email as a binary vector saying which words it contains.
- [75:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=4550s) With d binary features there are 2 to the d possible vectors, far too many to model directly when d is in the tens of thousands.
- [76:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=4613s) The naive Bayes assumption: given the class, the words are independent. It is clearly false (for example negation, as in not good), but it makes the model tractable.
- [78:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=4708s) This reduces the number of parameters to order d (2d plus 1) instead of exponential in d.
- [79:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=4741s) Estimates are counts: the spam fraction, and for each word the fraction of spam emails that contain it. This is maximum likelihood, cheap even on huge corpora, and prediction adds log probabilities.
- [80:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRdE8A4UZes&t=4805s) Laplace smoothing adds one synthetic count to avoid zero probabilities and makes the model less confident, a first example of regularization.

### Formulas

Generative classification uses Bayes' rule (the denominator does not depend on y):

$$
p(y\mid x)=\frac{p(x\mid y)\,p(y)}{p(x)},\qquad
\hat y=\arg\max_y\; p(x\mid y)\,p(y)
$$

Multivariate Gaussian with mean vector mu and covariance matrix Sigma in d dimensions:

$$
p(x;\mu,\Sigma)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\Big)
$$

The GDA model:

$$
y\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\phi),\qquad x\mid y=0\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_0,\Sigma),\qquad x\mid y=1\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\Sigma)
$$

Maximum likelihood estimates for GDA:

$$
\hat\phi=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{y^{(i)}=1\},\qquad
\hat\mu_k=\frac{\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}\{y^{(i)}=k\}\,x^{(i)}}{\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}\{y^{(i)}=k\}},\qquad
\hat\Sigma=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(x^{(i)}-\hat\mu_{y^{(i)}}\big)\big(x^{(i)}-\hat\mu_{y^{(i)}}\big)^\top
$$

The GDA posterior is logistic, with a linear score:

$$
p(y=1\mid x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(w^\top x+b)}},\quad
w=\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0),\quad
b=-\tfrac12\big(\mu_1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu_1-\mu_0^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu_0\big)+\log\frac{\phi}{1-\phi}
$$

Naive Bayes assumption, its classifier, and the Laplace smoothed estimate for a binary word feature:

$$
p(x_1,\dots,x_d\mid y)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}p(x_j\mid y),\qquad
\hat y=\arg\max_y\Big[\log p(y)+\sum_{j}\log p(x_j\mid y)\Big],\qquad
\hat\phi_{j\mid y}=\frac{\#\{\text{emails in class }y\text{ containing word }j\}+1}{\#\{\text{emails in class }y\}+2}
$$

## Lecture 6: Bias, variance, regularization and model selection

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 6](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ). The YouTube title (Dataset Split, ML Advice) covers only the end of the lecture

This lecture asks how to choose a model that generalizes from a finite, noisy sample. It builds the picture of underfitting (high bias) and overfitting (high variance) with polynomial fits, derives the bias variance decomposition of squared error, and introduces regularization through ridge regression as a way to trade a little bias for a large drop in variance. The lecturer then adds two modern complications: double descent, where huge interpolating models generalize anyway, and the ImageNet v2 study showing that years of reuse of a test set did not cause the overfitting that statisticians feared. The lecture closes with practical model selection: train, dev and test splits, k fold cross validation, and the successive halving idea behind Hyperband.

### Key ideas

- [0:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=5s) The lecture covers bias and variance, regularization and model selection, plus two modern additions: double descent and a study of adaptive overfitting on ImageNet.
- [2:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=163s) The central question of machine learning: given a finite noisy sample, how do we choose a model that generalizes to future data?
- [3:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=226s) Setup: a true function h star (here a quadratic) generates data, and we observe only y equal to h star of x plus Gaussian noise.
- [5:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=321s) The training set is treated as a random sample from a population. Imagine three different training sets S1, S2 and S3 drawn from the same world, and fit a model to each.
- [7:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=420s) Underfitting: a straight line fit to quadratic data always has a systematic error, whatever sample you use. This is high bias, and the fix is a more expressive model.
- [8:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=516s) Overfitting: a very high degree polynomial can interpolate each training sample with zero error, but the fitted curve changes wildly from sample to sample. This is high variance.
- [10:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=643s) A high variance model adapts to the noise rather than the signal, and the same happens in high dimensional linear models.
- [11:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=706s) A right model class has an inductive bias that matches the problem: the degrees of freedom roughly match the phenomenon, so fits are similar across samples and stay close to the data.
- [13:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=801s) The classical curve: as complexity grows bias falls and variance rises, so test error is U shaped.
- [14:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=866s) Interpolation means fitting the training set with zero loss. Large deep networks interpolate yet still generalize, which is surprising from the classical view.
- [17:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=1024s) Training error mainly reflects bias, while test error also contains variance and noise.
- [18:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=1086s) Classification has the same problem in high dimensions: small islands of one class inside another are a sign of overfitting.
- [20:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=1213s) The thought experiment for the decomposition: fix a test point x, draw a random training set S, train h_S, draw a noisy test label, and average the squared error over both sources of randomness.
- [26:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=1571s) The two sources of randomness, the training sample and the test noise, are independent. h_S is a complicated random variable, but only its expectation is needed.
- [31:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=1919s) Expanding the square, the test error equals the irreducible noise sigma squared plus the gap between h star and h_S. The cross term vanishes because the noise has zero mean and is independent of S.
- [33:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2013s) Define the long run average prediction h bar of x as the expectation of h_S of x over training sets.
- [35:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2108s) The remaining gap splits into squared bias (h star minus h bar, squared) and variance (the spread of h_S around h bar).
- [36:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2171s) Bias is a property of the model class and ignores the sample. Variance measures how much predictions jump between samples. Noise is inevitable.
- [36:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2203s) To reduce variance, use a smaller model, or take a large model and regularize it so it does not jump around.
- [37:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2234s) The decomposition shown is for squared loss regression with Gaussian noise, but the same idea applies widely.
- [41:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2486s) A development (held out) set estimates the combined effect of bias and variance without touching the test set.
- [42:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2549s) Regularization adds a little bias to reduce variance a lot. It can also be seen as encoding prior knowledge, for example that coefficients should not be large.
- [43:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2612s) Ridge regression adds a penalty on the squared size of theta to the least squares cost, with weight rho.
- [44:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2676s) Other penalties are possible (sparse, or other norms). The squared norm is chosen because the math stays clean.
- [45:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2708s) Weight decay and dropout in deep learning can be interpreted as forms of L2 regularization.
- [45:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2741s) With rho equal to zero you get ordinary least squares. As rho grows, theta shrinks toward zero. A middle value balances fit and size and is chosen on development data.
- [47:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2869s) When there are fewer examples than features, X transpose X is not invertible and an entire null space of equally good solutions exists.
- [48:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2903s) The ridge penalty charges for any component in the null space, so it picks the minimum norm solution.
- [49:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=2997s) The ridge solution has a closed form: the inverse of X transpose X plus rho times the identity, applied to X transpose y.
- [51:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3062s) Adding rho shifts every eigenvalue of X transpose X (all nonnegative) to at least rho, so the matrix is always invertible.
- [52:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3131s) Even with full rank, tiny eigenvalues make the solution unstable. Ridge lifts them, giving a more stable optimization and lower variance.
- [54:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3259s) The trade: a small increase in bias (the lecturer's example is of order 0.001 to 0.1) buys a massive reduction in variance.
- [57:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3455s) Numerical precision matters: machine learning trains in low precision formats such as FP8, where instabilities accumulate, so regularization constants are often set by heuristics.
- [58:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3518s) Other ways to prevent fitting noise: data augmentation (perturb the inputs), layer normalization, dropout. Many tricks can be read as regularization.
- [60:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3613s) Choosing rho is mostly trial and error on a development set. Standardizing the data first helps when features have different scales.
- [61:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3676s) The L1 norm is convex and gives sparse solutions, while the L0 norm that counts nonzero entries is not convex.
- [62:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3770s) Classical statistics says test error should explode for a polynomial that interpolates a few points. Yet modern models have many more parameters than training points, interpolate, and still generalize.
- [64:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3865s) Double descent: past the interpolation threshold, test error falls again as capacity grows.
- [64:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=3898s) There are many zero loss solutions, and the optimizer tends to pick a smooth one, an effect called implicit bias.
- [67:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4024s) Adaptive overfitting is the worry that reusing a test set leaks information, as in p hacking. It is why clinical trials must be registered in advance.
- [67:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4056s) The ImageNet v2 study rebuilt a test set from scratch, ten years later, to check whether years of reuse of the original test set had misled the field.
- [68:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4088s) Accuracy dropped by a similar amount for every model, but the ranking of models stayed almost unchanged, so adaptive overfitting was smaller than feared.
- [69:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4182s) Implicit bias appears even in least squares: the pseudoinverse or SGD from a small start picks a minimum norm solution among many.
- [71:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4276s) A held out test set is sacred. Use a development set to pick hyperparameters, and give as much data as you can to training.
- [71:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4308s) Choosing hyperparameters on the training set would always favor the most expressive model, so a separate set is needed.
- [72:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4339s) Cross validation uses data efficiently: split into k parts (for example five), train on k minus 1, evaluate on the remaining part, and rotate.
- [73:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4402s) Hyperband or successive halving: run all configurations briefly, drop the worst half, give the survivors twice the budget, and repeat.
- [74:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4497s) With eight configurations of a model that takes two days, training them all takes 16 days, while successive halving finds the best for a fraction of that.
- [77:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llnEgyyuYkQ&t=4654s) Modern models live in a mode beyond the U shaped curve. Double descent and robustness to test set reuse show that much about generalization is still not understood.

### Formulas

Data model, with true function h star and Gaussian noise:

$$
y=h^\star(x)+\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)
$$

Average predictor over random training sets S:

$$
\bar h(x)=\mathbb{E}_S\big[h_S(x)\big]
$$

Bias variance decomposition of the expected squared error at a test point x:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[(h_S(x)-y)^2\big]=\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}
+\underbrace{\big(h^\star(x)-\bar h(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_S\big[(\bar h(x)-h_S(x))^2\big]}_{\text{variance}}
$$

Ridge regression cost and solution:

$$
J_\rho(\theta)=\tfrac12\|X\theta-y\|_2^2+\tfrac{\rho}{2}\|\theta\|_2^2,\qquad
\hat\theta_\rho=\big(X^\top X+\rho I\big)^{-1}X^\top y
$$

Cross validation estimate with k folds, where the model for fold j is trained without that fold:

$$
\text{CV}(\rho)=\frac1k\sum_{j=1}^{k}\operatorname{err}_j\big(\hat\theta^{(-j)}_\rho\big)
$$

## Lecture 7: Neural network architectures and normalization

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 7](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo)

This lecture opens the deep learning block by extending the supervised learning framework to nonlinear models. It restates the loss and optimizer recipe (squared error for regression, softmax with cross entropy for classification, gradient descent and SGD) and argues why SGD works even though the loss is not convex. It then builds neural networks from the ground up: ReLU and other activation functions, neurons and layers written as matrix multiplications, stacking layers into multilayer perceptrons with compatible dimensions, residual connections, and layer normalization. Convolutional layers are mentioned briefly as matrices with shared structure. Computing gradients through these models by backpropagation is left to the next lecture.

### Key ideas

- [0:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=5s) These two lectures lay the foundations of deep learning for supervised learning: a framework for nonlinear models, neural network architectures, and (next lecture) backpropagation for computing gradients.
- [2:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=169s) A model that is nonlinear only in the inputs, such as theta_1 x_1 squared plus theta_2 x_2 squared, is not nonlinear enough: replace x squared by a new feature z and it is a linear model.
- [3:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=233s) The interesting case is nonlinearity in the parameters, for example theta_1 squared times x_1, which cannot be turned into a linear model and needs different techniques.
- [8:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=493s) The framework for regression: labels are real numbers, the model h_theta maps R^d to R, and the loss for each example is the squared difference between prediction and label.
- [9:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=557s) The loss is a function of theta (the data is given), and the overall loss is the average of the per example losses, which we minimize over theta.
- [11:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=685s) For multi class classification the model outputs K numbers called logits, one per class.
- [11:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=718s) Softmax turns logits into probabilities: exponentiate each logit and divide by the sum of the exponentials.
- [13:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=782s) Training minimizes the negative log likelihood of the correct label, which is the cross entropy loss. In PyTorch it is the CrossEntropyLoss module.
- [16:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=984s) It is called cross entropy because, averaged over labels from the true distribution, it equals the cross entropy between the true distribution and the model's distribution.
- [18:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=1111s) Under a Gaussian noise assumption, the negative log likelihood of a regression model is the squared error up to constants, so scaling does not change the optimization problem.
- [21:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=1277s) Newton's method cannot be relied on because the loss is not convex, and no method is guaranteed for all non convex functions. Stochastic gradient descent is the practical choice.
- [21:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=1309s) Gradient descent uses the gradient of the average loss, while SGD uses the gradient of a randomly chosen example, updating theta by minus the step size times that gradient.
- [23:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=1408s) On average the single example gradient equals the full gradient, so SGD follows gradient descent in expectation, with noise that can occasionally point the wrong way.
- [26:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=1567s) Why use SGD: the full gradient is very expensive (about 1 million examples in 2015, about 1 trillion tokens in 2026), individual gradients are highly correlated so a subset suffices, and exactness is not needed.
- [28:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=1696s) The community does not believe the noise helps SGD escape bad local minima. The more accepted view is that high dimensional losses have very few bad local minima, perhaps none.
- [31:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=1887s) In high dimensions, a point with zero gradient and a Hessian that is positive semidefinite in every direction is a demanding condition, so most stationary points have some downhill direction.
- [33:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=1982s) In practice you use minibatches of B examples: less noise than a single example and better GPU utilization, since a single example leaves most of the hardware idle.
- [35:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=2118s) Two questions remain: how to define the model h_theta, and how to compute the gradient of the loss for each example (backpropagation, next lecture).
- [37:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=2246s) Motivation: housing prices as a function of size probably start flat and then grow linearly. A linear model cannot produce such a curve.
- [38:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=2281s) Such a curve can be written as max of (w x plus b) and 0, plus a constant c, which cuts off everything below zero.
- [39:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=2346s) The function max(t, 0) is called the rectified linear unit, or ReLU, and is an activation function, named by loose analogy with neurons firing above a threshold. This is already a one dimensional neural network.
- [41:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=2476s) The sigmoid was used at the start of deep learning but turned out to be a bad idea for computational reasons.
- [42:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=2544s) For d dimensional input, a neuron computes ReLU of (w dot x plus b), where w is a weight vector and b is a scalar offset, the bias.
- [43:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=2608s) Intermediate features such as walkability or school quality are not in the raw data but matter for price. Each can be a nonlinear function of the inputs, and the output is a linear combination of them.
- [45:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=2737s) Stacking two layers: first compute m neurons from the input, then output a linear combination of them. Composition of linear maps and nonlinearities is the fundamental idea.
- [51:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=3080s) In matrix form, a layer is ReLU applied elementwise to (W x plus b), where W is m by d and its rows are the weight vectors of the m neurons.
- [53:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=3213s) A layer from d inputs to m outputs has m times d weights plus m biases.
- [55:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=3356s) Other activations map R to R and are applied elementwise: sigmoid, tanh, leaky ReLU, GELU. They are mostly increasing, smooth functions.
- [58:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=3485s) Why one activation beats another is partly magic and trial and error. ReLU is not smooth, so smoother variants are often used.
- [58:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=3518s) A network is a composition of layers, each a matrix multiplication followed by an activation, and the number of layers is the number of such transformations.
- [60:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=3614s) Each layer has its own W and b. Dimensions must be compatible: the input dimension of W must equal the output dimension of the previous layer, while the output dimension can be chosen freely.
- [62:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=3779s) An MLP (multilayer perceptron) is a block of one or a few matrix multiplications with activations, and architectures are often drawn as boxes of such blocks.
- [64:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=3844s) Intermediate dimensions usually shrink relative to the input: for language the input (vocabulary) might be around 250 thousand dimensions and is squeezed to about 2 thousand, and images are compressed similarly.
- [65:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=3942s) A residual block applies a small network (two matrix multiply and activation steps) and adds the input back. Input and output dimensions must match. It was introduced in the 2015 residual network paper.
- [66:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4005s) A residual network applies many residual blocks, with an initial step to change dimension and possibly a final one to change it back.
- [67:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4068s) Motivation: if the representation is already close to the target, model the difference instead. Writing y equal to z plus a small network of z gives the residual form.
- [68:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4132s) Using two layers inside the block works better than one, and two or three layers seem best, for reasons not fully understood.
- [70:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4227s) Other explanations are optimization based: adding the identity can make optimization easier. It is hard to know which explanation is correct.
- [72:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4323s) Layer normalization normalizes a vector: subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation across its coordinates.
- [73:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4420s) Learnable parameters gamma and beta let the network choose the scale and shift after normalizing.
- [74:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4459s) RMS normalization is a simpler variant used in modern large language models: it only divides by the root mean square, without subtracting the mean.
- [77:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4623s) Normalization makes the layer scale invariant: normalizing alpha times z gives the same result as normalizing z.
- [78:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4689s) This stabilizes training: without normalization, repeated layers can make activations grow enormously (for example 10 to the 20th) and blow up. It also makes initialization easier.
- [78:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4720s) A subtlety: although the function is scale invariant, its gradient is not, which creates optimization side effects.
- [79:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4752s) A convolutional layer is a matrix with a special structure (a Toeplitz matrix) where the same filter vector is shifted across rows, so weights are shared.
- [79:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fRM41w9jzQo&t=4784s) Convolutional networks are less used now except in specific computer vision cases, since transformers often replace them.

### Formulas

Regression loss for one example, and the overall loss that is minimized over theta:

$$
J^{(i)}(\theta)=\big(y^{(i)}-h_\theta(x^{(i)})\big)^2,\qquad
J(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}J^{(i)}(\theta)
$$

Softmax over the K logits of the model, and the cross entropy loss for a label y:

$$
P(y=j\mid x)=\frac{\exp\big(h_\theta(x)_j\big)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\big(h_\theta(x)_k\big)},\qquad
J^{(i)}(\theta)=-\log\frac{\exp\big(h_\theta(x^{(i)})_{y^{(i)}}\big)}{\sum_{k}\exp\big(h_\theta(x^{(i)})_k\big)}
$$

Gradient descent and SGD updates:

$$
\theta:=\theta-\eta\,\nabla J(\theta),\qquad
\theta:=\theta-\eta\,\nabla J^{(j)}(\theta),\quad j\sim\text{Uniform}\{1,\dots,n\},\qquad
\mathbb{E}_j\big[\nabla J^{(j)}(\theta)\big]=\nabla J(\theta)
$$

ReLU, a layer with m neurons, and its parameter count:

$$
\operatorname{ReLU}(t)=\max(t,0),\qquad a=\operatorname{ReLU}(Wx+b),\quad W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d},\ b\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\qquad
\#\text{params}=md+m
$$

A two layer network and a general deep network, with an activation sigma:

$$
h_\theta(x)=w^{[2]\top}\operatorname{ReLU}\big(W^{[1]}x+b^{[1]}\big)+b^{[2]},\qquad
h_\theta(x)=W^{[L]}\sigma\Big(\cdots\sigma\big(W^{[1]}x+b^{[1]}\big)\cdots\Big)+b^{[L]}
$$

A residual block (the small network F has two matrix multiply and activation steps, and its output dimension equals the input dimension):

$$
\operatorname{Res}(z)=z+F(z)
$$

Layer normalization and RMS normalization (elementwise gamma and beta, m coordinates):

$$
\hat\mu=\tfrac1m\sum_i z_i,\quad \hat\sigma^2=\tfrac1m\sum_i(z_i-\hat\mu)^2,\quad
\operatorname{LN}(z)=\beta+\gamma\odot\frac{z-\hat\mu}{\hat\sigma},\qquad
\operatorname{RMSNorm}(z)=\gamma\odot\frac{z}{\sqrt{\tfrac1m\sum_i z_i^2}}
$$

## Lecture 8: Backpropagation and automatic differentiation

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8)

This lecture explains how the gradient of a network's loss with respect to all its parameters is computed efficiently. It starts with a general result: any scalar function computed by a circuit of N elementary operations has a gradient computable in about the same O(N) time, so the backward pass costs about as much as the forward pass. It then builds backpropagation from the chain rule, seen as a backward function that turns the gradient with respect to a module's output into gradients with respect to its input and parameters, using only local information. The lecture derives the backward functions for matrix multiplication and for elementwise activations, and mentions advanced uses such as Hessian vector products and differentiating through an optimization step.

### Key ideas

- [0:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=5s) Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters, which SGD then uses to update theta.
- [1:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=102s) The key question: how do we compute the gradient of a very complex multilayer network efficiently?
- [2:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=135s) Backpropagation is one of the most technical questions in deep learning and long a standard interview question in research labs, since most other choices (learning rate, data size) are subjective.
- [2:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=167s) It is also called automatic differentiation and can be done by software, but understanding it matters because many of your decisions depend on how it works.
- [3:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=234s) Automatic differentiation is not limited to neural networks. It applies to any function you can define as a straight line sequence of elementary operations.
- [5:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=309s) Such a function is a circuit or computational graph built from arithmetic operations (add, multiply, divide) and elementary functions (sine, exponential, sigmoid). Unlike a formula, a graph can reuse intermediate results.
- [6:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=408s) Informal theorem: if a differentiable circuit of size N computes a scalar valued function f, then the gradient of f with respect to all its inputs can also be computed in O(N) time.
- [9:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=571s) In deep learning terms, the forward pass computes the function and the backward pass computes the gradient, and the two take about the same time.
- [11:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=666s) For almost all networks the number of operations is proportional to the number of parameters (a d by d matrix has d squared parameters and a matrix vector product costs d squared operations). So the loss and its gradient cost order the number of parameters.
- [12:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=761s) The same idea can be applied twice to get some second order information.
- [13:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=825s) Example: the loss after one gradient step, J of (theta minus eta times the gradient), can itself be differentiated with respect to theta efficiently.
- [15:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=920s) Such tricks are used in meta learning, for example tuning a learning rate or an initialization by backpropagating through the training algorithm.
- [16:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=992s) Hessian vector products: define g(x) as the dot product of the gradient of f with a vector v. The gradient of g is the Hessian times v, so it costs O(n) even though the full n by n Hessian cannot be formed.
- [18:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=1119s) AdamW does not use second order derivatives. Using second order information needs fairly advanced optimizers.
- [20:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=1217s) The chain rule seen as a backward step: given the gradient of the final output with respect to an intermediate variable u, compute the gradient with respect to the earlier variable z that produced u.
- [22:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=1348s) Written out, the derivative with respect to z_i is a sum over the intermediate variables u_j of the derivative with respect to u_j times the derivative of u_j with respect to z_i.
- [23:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=1380s) As a matrix operation, the gradient with respect to z is the transposed Jacobian of the function G, multiplied by the gradient with respect to u.
- [24:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=1497s) Only G and the value of z are needed. The rest of the computation (the function F after u) can be forgotten, which is what makes backpropagation efficient and modular.
- [26:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=1591s) In PyTorch, each module implements both a forward function and a backward function. The backward function only needs local information.
- [28:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=1723s) For a chain of modules, you first compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the last output (a local step), then apply each module's backward function in turn going backward.
- [32:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=1925s) Modules can all be different (matrix multiplication, sigmoid, layer normalization), each with its own kind of Jacobian.
- [33:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=2021s) We usually want derivatives with respect to the parameters, not the data. Treat each module as a function of its parameters with the input held fixed, and apply the same backward rule.
- [36:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=2185s) Backpropagation has two interleaved phases: pass backward through the activations to get the gradient at each intermediate variable, and from each of those, branch off to get the gradient of that layer's parameters.
- [40:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=2458s) The gradient for the first layer's parameters needs the whole backward chain, but later layers' parameter gradients can be computed in parallel while the chain continues.
- [41:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=2490s) Once a layer's gradients are computed you can discard stored values to free memory. The order of computation and the dependency graph determine memory use and GPU communication.
- [43:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=2603s) A concrete module: u equals W z plus b, with W of shape n by m.
- [47:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=2878s) Backward through matrix multiplication: the gradient with respect to z is W transpose times the gradient with respect to u. The backward function is the transposed matrix.
- [50:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=3041s) Gradient with respect to a weight W_ij: the only term that survives is the gradient of u_i times z_j.
- [52:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=3144s) So the gradient of W is the outer product of the upstream gradient with the input z transpose, which in biology is called Hebb's rule: the error at one end of an edge times the signal at the other end.
- [54:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=3283s) For a single example, the gradient of a weight matrix is rank one (an outer product), a fact used in some efficient methods. This no longer holds after averaging over many examples.
- [56:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=3384s) Backward through an activation u equals sigma of z: the Jacobian is diagonal, because each output depends only on its own input.
- [58:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=3481s) So the backward step is an elementwise product of the upstream gradient with the derivative of sigma at z. There is no need to form the diagonal matrix.
- [59:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=3545s) Efficiency check: a matrix multiplication of an m by n matrix costs about mn forward, and the backward pass (transpose product and outer product for the weight gradient) also costs about mn.
- [60:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=3610s) For an elementwise activation both the forward and backward passes cost order m.
- [61:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ne2ngVAoMG8&t=3673s) Modern activations include GELU and SwiGLU, which uses gating by multiplying two matrix applications elementwise. Any one dimensional function that is quick to differentiate works.

### Formulas

Chain rule as a backward function: if u = G(z) and J depends on z only through u, then, with the Jacobian of G, the gradient with respect to z follows from the gradient with respect to u:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_i}=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial J}{\partial u_j}\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial z_i},\qquad
\frac{\partial J}{\partial z}=\Big(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\Big)^{\top}\frac{\partial J}{\partial u}
$$

Backward through a matrix multiplication module u = Wz + b (with delta equal to the gradient of J with respect to u):

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial z}=W^\top\delta,\qquad
\frac{\partial J}{\partial W}=\delta\,z^\top,\qquad
\frac{\partial J}{\partial b}=\delta,\qquad \delta=\frac{\partial J}{\partial u}
$$

Backward through an elementwise activation u = sigma(z) (a diagonal Jacobian, so an elementwise product):

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial z}=\sigma'(z)\odot\frac{\partial J}{\partial u}
$$

Softmax cross entropy at the output, for logits z and one hot label e_y:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial z}=p-e_y,\qquad p=\operatorname{softmax}(z)
$$

Cheap gradient principle, and the Hessian vector product:

$$
\text{time}(\nabla f)=O\big(\text{time}(f)\big)=O(N),\qquad
g(x)=\nabla f(x)^\top v\;\Rightarrow\;\nabla g(x)=\nabla^2 f(x)\,v
$$

## Lecture 9: K-means, Gaussian mixtures and the EM idea

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA). The YouTube title says (non-EM), but the last third of the lecture introduces the E and M steps and sets up EM through Jensen's inequality

This lecture starts the unsupervised learning block with the two most common clustering algorithms. K-means alternates between assigning each point to its nearest center and moving each center to the mean of its points, and the lecture explains why it terminates, why it can only find local minima, how K-means++ initialization helps, and why choosing K is a modeling decision. It then introduces the Gaussian mixture model (GMM), a probabilistic version with a hidden source variable for each point, and sketches the EM algorithm: an E step that computes soft assignments with Bayes' rule and an M step that re estimates parameters as weighted averages. The lecture closes with convexity and Jensen's inequality, and a picture of EM as repeatedly maximizing a lower bound on the likelihood.

### Key ideas

- [0:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=37s) Learning without labels leads to K-means and Gaussian mixture models, which are closely related. They also lead to the expectation maximization (EM) idea, a classic general algorithm for structure you only weakly know.
- [3:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=197s) With no labels there is no canonical goal like separating positives from negatives. We have to model the data's structure, which means stronger assumptions and weaker guarantees.
- [6:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=386s) K-means with K clusters: assign each point an integer c_i in 1 to K saying which cluster it belongs to.
- [8:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=513s) Algorithm: initialize K centers at random, then repeat two steps. Assign each point to the nearest center, then move each center to the mean of the points assigned to it.
- [11:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=676s) Closeness is measured with squared Euclidean distance, used for convenience.
- [11:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=708s) It does not matter which center is called mu1 or mu2. Without labels, only the positions of the centers matter.
- [13:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=803s) The algorithm stops when assignments no longer change, so the centers stop moving.
- [14:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=868s) Does it terminate? Yes in practice: the sum of squared distances to the assigned centers decreases monotonically, though it says nothing about how fast.
- [16:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=995s) Finding the global optimum of the K-means objective is NP hard, so the algorithm cannot reliably find it. It can be slow or settle on a wrong answer.
- [17:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1059s) There can be many local minima, so the random initialization matters.
- [18:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1122s) K-means++ chooses the initial centers by weighting, which provably gives a good approximation to the optimum whatever the data. It was invented by Stanford graduate students and is the default in scikit-learn.
- [20:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1247s) Practical stopping rules: a fixed number of iterations or when the clusters stabilize.
- [21:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1314s) In high dimensions, strange things happen (nearest and farthest neighbors are about equally far), so K-means on structureless data returns a result that is not meaningful.
- [22:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1377s) K-means is meaningful only if the data has real structure, such as gene expression types or stars with distinct emission. Unsupervised learning mostly gives descriptive insight that needs checking some other way.
- [24:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1440s) Choosing K is a modeling assumption about how many clusters there are. The same points can reasonably have two or four clusters, and three can feel odd.
- [25:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1537s) You can run different initializations and different K, but comparing the K-means objective across K fails because it keeps decreasing as K grows.
- [28:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1697s) There is no great quality indicator for K-means. Probabilistic models (GMM) give a likelihood, and PCA has a variance explained test, which comes next.
- [29:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1790s) The Gaussian mixture model is K-means with probability and Bayes' rule: soft assignments instead of hard ones.
- [30:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1821s) Example: photons arriving from the sky come from different sources (such as stars and quasars) with different intensities and shapes, and we want to assign each photon to a source.
- [31:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=1918s) Instead of a hard cluster, give each point a probability for each source. Points near a boundary can be 60 percent one source and 40 percent another.
- [33:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=2023s) Assumptions: the number of sources K is known, sources are Gaussian with their own means and covariances, and the number of points per source is unknown and learned.
- [36:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=2216s) Generative story: flip a biased coin (for example 60 and 40 percent) to pick a source, then draw a point from that source's Gaussian.
- [38:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=2312s) If the labels were known, this would be GDA. The new part is that the labels are missing.
- [40:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=2439s) Goal: output for each point i and source j the soft assignment, the probability that x_i came from source j.
- [41:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=2504s) The model: z_i is a latent (hidden, unobserved) source chosen from a multinomial distribution with probabilities phi, and given z_i, x_i is Gaussian with that source's mean and variance.
- [43:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=2600s) Phi is not a known prior here. It is a parameter that is learned from the data.
- [45:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=2727s) The likelihood of the data under the model is a function of phi, the means and the variances, so the parameters can be estimated from the observed points alone.
- [47:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=2824s) Intuition for the role of phi: if the second source produces 100 times more data, a point on the boundary is more likely from it. This reasoning is Bayes' rule.
- [48:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=2920s) The probability of a point is a mixture: the sum over sources of the probability of the source times the Gaussian density for that source.
- [50:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=3018s) EM in outline: the E step guesses the latent variable for each point (soft assignments), and the M step updates all parameters given those guesses.
- [51:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=3081s) E step: for each point and source, the soft assignment is computed with Bayes' rule, from the current parameters, as the joint probability divided by the sum over sources.
- [54:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=3240s) M step: the weight of source j is the average of its soft assignments, and its mean is the weighted average of the points, using the soft assignments as weights. Covariances are weighted too.
- [55:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=3337s) If the soft assignments were all 0 or 1, the M step would be exactly the K-means centroid update.
- [57:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=3433s) Repeat E and M steps. The process is similar to K-means: initialize centers, move them, and reassign points.
- [57:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=3465s) Intuitively each iteration finds the shape and location of every source and then uses them to re evaluate each point. A source that becomes very narrow in one direction can push far points away.
- [59:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=3565s) To justify EM we need Jensen's inequality, which turns the hard log likelihood into a simpler surrogate.
- [61:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=3665s) A set is convex if the line between any two of its points stays inside it. A function is convex if its graph lies below the chord joining any two points, equivalently its epigraph is a convex set.
- [64:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=3863s) If f is twice differentiable and f double prime is nonnegative everywhere, f is convex. This follows from Taylor's theorem with remainder.
- [67:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=4027s) Jensen's inequality: for a convex f, the expectation of f(X) is at least f of the expectation. It is just the definition of convexity, extended by induction.
- [68:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=4092s) A function is concave if its negative is convex, such as minus x squared or the logarithm. For concave functions, Jensen's inequality reverses.
- [69:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=4185s) The log likelihood is a sum over examples of the log of a sum over the hidden variable, and EM handles it by maximizing a surrogate.
- [71:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=4283s) EM idea: at the current theta_t, build a lower bound L_t(theta) that lies below the log likelihood everywhere and touches it at theta_t. Maximize the bound to get theta_t+1.
- [72:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=4378s) Properties: L_t(theta) is at most L(theta) for every theta, and equals it at theta_t.
- [74:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSmIGBCoffA&t=4478s) The E step constructs the bound and the M step maximizes it. This increases the likelihood each round but does not find the global maximum, as with K-means.

### Formulas

K-means objective and the two alternating steps (c_i is the cluster of point i, mu_j the center of cluster j):

$$
J(c,\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|x^{(i)}-\mu_{c^{(i)}}\big\|^2,\qquad
c^{(i)}:=\arg\min_{j}\big\|x^{(i)}-\mu_j\big\|^2,\qquad
\mu_j:=\frac{\sum_i\mathbf{1}\{c^{(i)}=j\}\,x^{(i)}}{\sum_i\mathbf{1}\{c^{(i)}=j\}}
$$

Gaussian mixture model with a latent source z for each point:

$$
z^{(i)}\sim\text{Multinomial}(\phi),\qquad x^{(i)}\mid z^{(i)}=j\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}(\mu_j,\Sigma_j)
$$

Marginal likelihood of the observed data under the mixture:

$$
p(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\phi_j\,\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_j,\Sigma_j),\qquad
\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sum_{j=1}^{K}\phi_j\,\mathcal{N}\big(x^{(i)};\mu_j,\Sigma_j\big)
$$

E step, soft assignments by Bayes' rule:

$$
w_{ij}=P\big(z^{(i)}=j\mid x^{(i)};\theta\big)=\frac{\phi_j\,\mathcal{N}\big(x^{(i)};\mu_j,\Sigma_j\big)}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\phi_l\,\mathcal{N}\big(x^{(i)};\mu_l,\Sigma_l\big)}
$$

M step, weighted re estimates:

$$
\phi_j=\frac1n\sum_{i}w_{ij},\qquad
\mu_j=\frac{\sum_iw_{ij}\,x^{(i)}}{\sum_iw_{ij}},\qquad
\Sigma_j=\frac{\sum_iw_{ij}\,\big(x^{(i)}-\mu_j\big)\big(x^{(i)}-\mu_j\big)^\top}{\sum_iw_{ij}}
$$

Convexity and Jensen's inequality (the direction reverses for concave functions such as the logarithm):

$$
f(\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b)\le\lambda f(a)+(1-\lambda)f(b),\qquad
f(\mathbb{E}X)\le\mathbb{E}f(X)\ \ (f\text{ convex}),\qquad
\mathbb{E}\log X\le\log\mathbb{E}X
$$

The lower bound idea behind EM (L is the log likelihood, L_t the surrogate built at theta_t):

$$
L_t(\theta)\le L(\theta)\ \ \forall\theta,\quad L_t(\theta_t)=L(\theta_t),\qquad
\theta_{t+1}=\arg\max_\theta L_t(\theta)\;\Rightarrow\; L(\theta_{t+1})\ge L(\theta_t)
$$

## Lecture 10: EM derivation and principal component analysis

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s)

This lecture first derives the EM algorithm properly. A trick with an auxiliary distribution Q over the hidden variable plus Jensen's inequality produces a lower bound on the log likelihood (the ELBO) that touches the true curve at the current parameters, and choosing Q as the posterior over the hidden variable makes the bound tight. The E step builds the bound and the M step maximizes it, which recovers the ad hoc Gaussian mixture updates and guarantees the likelihood never decreases, though only to a local maximum. The second half introduces principal component analysis (PCA) as the non probabilistic workhorse of dimensionality reduction. After centering and scaling the data, the principal components are the top eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and the lecture warns what goes wrong with unscaled data, near equal eigenvalues and missing data.

### Key ideas

- [0:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=36s) PCA is a real workhorse for data analysis, and EM is the general fallback for latent variable models. Today covers the EM derivation and then PCA.
- [1:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=104s) Recap of GMM: each point has a hidden variable z_i saying which source it came from, assigned softly, and the parameters (means, covariances, mixing weights phi) are re fit after each round.
- [6:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=373s) The picture of EM: theta is the vector of all parameters and L(theta) is the log likelihood curve with several hills. At the current theta_t, EM builds a surrogate curve L_t.
- [8:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=509s) The surrogate is tight (it equals L at theta_t) and lies below L everywhere else. It is also concave and easy to maximize, so we climb it to get theta_t+1.
- [9:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=578s) The E step constructs the surrogate and the M step maximizes it, then we repeat. EM does not reach the global maximum, since L may have many hills.
- [11:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=710s) The compromise: the original function is nasty, but the local surrogate is nice. This is our first example of an alternating method, like K-means.
- [13:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=781s) To construct L_t, analyze one data point, since iid lets us sum over data points afterwards. The log likelihood contains a sum over the hidden variable z.
- [14:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=844s) Trick: multiply and divide by Q(z), any distribution over z, so the sum becomes an expectation over z drawn from Q of the ratio p(x, z) over Q(z).
- [15:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=943s) Now apply Jensen's inequality to the logarithm, which is concave: the log of an expectation is at least the expectation of the log. This is the lower bound.
- [16:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1009s) Each data point may choose its own Q_i, so this is not one Q for everyone.
- [17:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1044s) Progress is guaranteed because the surrogate is tight at the current point, so maximizing it cannot give a lower value of L than at theta_t.
- [18:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1110s) The reason for the Q trick is operational: it lets us treat the sum as an expected value and apply Jensen's inequality.
- [20:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1250s) Jensen becomes an equality when the ratio p(x, z; theta) over Q(z) is constant in z.
- [22:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1323s) Choose Q(z) as the conditional distribution of z given x at the current parameters. Then the ratio equals p(x; theta) for every z, so the bound is tight.
- [23:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1420s) Summing over data points, the lower bound is below L(theta) for every theta, and equal to it at theta_t. This is the evidence lower bound, the ELBO.
- [24:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1454s) The ELBO is used all the time in latent variable models, and diffusion models use it underneath.
- [26:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1617s) The assumption behind EM is strong: we must know the structure of the hidden variable and the form of the joint distribution p(x, z; theta) (here, point sources with Gaussian shapes).
- [29:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1748s) Z cannot be measured, but running the procedure guesses it: Q_i(z) is a soft assignment based on the data and the current parameters, like 50 percent source one and 50 percent source two.
- [31:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1878s) The E step computes these Q_i, and the M step maximizes the likelihood given them, a more advanced version of the K-means update.
- [32:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=1947s) EM is a local method. Nothing prevents it from missing a nearby hill, and gradient descent can also jump over minima.
- [33:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=2016s) E step for the mixture: the soft assignment is found with Bayes' rule, comparing the density of each source at the point and weighting by how common each source is.
- [38:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=2325s) If the second source had a thousand times more points, its weight would multiply its density and shift the soft assignment, which is what Bayes' rule says.
- [41:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=2491s) Why not run gradient descent directly? You would have to marginalize over the hidden variables. EM uses the structure of the model to alternate between easy steps.
- [44:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=2653s) M step for the means: take the derivative of the surrogate with respect to mu_j, set it to zero, and get a weighted average of the points.
- [45:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=2717s) The covariance update comes out the same way, a weighted average of the outer products, using that the covariance matrix has full rank.
- [47:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=2820s) The mixing weights phi are constrained to be nonnegative and sum to one, so deriving their update requires a Lagrange multiplier. Skipping it gives wrong answers.
- [48:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=2887s) The big picture: EM recovers the ad hoc algorithm. Q_i is the best guess of the clustering given the current parameters, Bayes' rule is exactly the choice that makes the bound tight.
- [49:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=2951s) Principal component analysis (PCA) is a workhorse for dimensionality reduction, visualization and feature building, and it can be computed cheaply even for large data.
- [49:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=2988s) Example: cars with city and highway fuel economy. The first principal component is the direction of greatest variation, roughly overall fuel efficiency.
- [51:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=3085s) Preprocessing step one is centering: subtract the mean so the data has mean zero. PCA looks for directions of maximum variance, and without centering the top direction points at the mean.
- [53:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=3188s) PCA finds orthonormal unit vectors u_1, u_2, and so on, where u_1 captures the most variability, u_2 the next most, and each point can be written in this basis.
- [54:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=3287s) Preprocessing step two is scaling each feature by its standard deviation, so units do not matter. Otherwise a feature measured in huge numbers (feet versus miles) would dominate.
- [55:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=3353s) In the cars example, the second component is roughly the difference between highway and city performance.
- [57:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=3428s) The closest point to a line through the origin in direction u (a unit vector) is alpha times u, where alpha is the dot product of u with x. This is an orthogonal projection.
- [60:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=3633s) With several orthonormal directions the closest point in their span is the sum of the projections onto each. Orthogonality makes this decomposition work.
- [61:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=3705s) The residual is the distance from the point to the line or subspace. Maximizing the projected variance is equivalent to minimizing the squared residuals.
- [62:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=3742s) So PCA maximizes the sum of squared projections of the data onto a unit direction u. Features on huge scales would dominate, which is why we normalize.
- [63:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=3809s) A symmetric matrix A has an eigendecomposition A equals U Lambda U transpose with orthonormal U and diagonal Lambda, with eigenvalues sorted from largest to smallest.
- [67:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4076s) Writing u in the eigenbasis, the quadratic form u transpose A u equals the sum of lambda_j times alpha_j squared, for unit alpha.
- [70:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4243s) The maximum over unit u is lambda_1, attained at u_1. So the principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, which the centering step makes appropriate.
- [71:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4317s) If the top two eigenvalues are equal, any direction in their span is a maximizer, so there is no single principal component. If they are close, the components are unstable.
- [73:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4384s) With close eigenvalues, rerunning PCA on new data can give different coordinates, so a model trained on the old coordinates may stop working.
- [74:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4457s) PCA fails if you do not center, if the features have very different scales, or if the eigenvalue spectrum is not well separated.
- [75:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4524s) Dimensionality reduction with PCA only makes sense when the kept eigenvalues are distinct and separated from the rest. The lecturer is not a big fan of using it as a blind feature reduction step.
- [75:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4556s) The total variance is the trace of the covariance matrix, and the variance captured by the top k directions is the sum of the top k eigenvalues, which can be computed without a full decomposition.
- [77:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4621s) Why reduce dimension: data often lives near a low dimensional subspace, so a few pseudo features explain most of it, which lets you draw stronger conclusions and compress.
- [78:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4685s) PCA assumes the structure is a subspace, while EM assumed hidden Gaussian sources. Both assumptions matter and need to be justified.
- [78:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sUS-eTa0l6s&t=4718s) As more components are added, the extra variance captured shrinks, so often a handful explain the dataset. Missing data is not handled by PCA and needs imputation or dropping.

### Formulas

Jensen's inequality for the log likelihood of one point, with any distribution Q over the hidden variable z (the lower bound):

$$
\log p(x;\theta)=\log\sum_{z}Q(z)\,\frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{Q(z)}\;\ge\;\sum_{z}Q(z)\log\frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{Q(z)}
$$

Equality holds when the ratio does not depend on z, which Q equal to the posterior achieves:

$$
Q(z)=p(z\mid x;\theta_t)\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{p(x,z;\theta_t)}{Q(z)}=p(x;\theta_t)\ \text{ for all }z
$$

The evidence lower bound (ELBO) summed over the data, and the EM steps:

$$
L(\theta)=\sum_{i}\log p\big(x^{(i)};\theta\big)\ \ge\ L_t(\theta)=\sum_{i}\sum_{z}Q_i(z)\log\frac{p\big(x^{(i)},z;\theta\big)}{Q_i(z)}
$$
$$
\text{E step: } Q_i(z)=p\big(z\mid x^{(i)};\theta_t\big),\qquad \text{M step: } \theta_{t+1}=\arg\max_\theta L_t(\theta)
$$

The likelihood never decreases:

$$
L(\theta_{t+1})\ \ge\ L_t(\theta_{t+1})\ \ge\ L_t(\theta_t)=L(\theta_t)
$$

Gaussian mixture M step with soft assignments w_ij (the mixing weights need a Lagrange multiplier for the sum to one constraint):

$$
\mu_j=\frac{\sum_iw_{ij}\,x^{(i)}}{\sum_iw_{ij}},\qquad
\phi_j=\frac1n\sum_{i}w_{ij}
$$

Projection of x onto a unit direction u, and onto the span of orthonormal directions u_1 to u_k, and the Pythagorean split of the squared length:

$$
\operatorname{proj}_u(x)=(u^\top x)\,u,\qquad
\operatorname{proj}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(u_j^\top x)\,u_j,\qquad
\|x\|^2=\|\operatorname{proj}(x)\|^2+\|x-\operatorname{proj}(x)\|^2
$$

PCA on centered (and scaled) data: first component and eigen decomposition of the covariance matrix:

$$
u_1=\arg\max_{\|u\|=1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(u^\top x^{(i)}\big)^2=\arg\max_{\|u\|=1}u^\top\hat\Sigma u,\qquad
\hat\Sigma=\frac1n\sum_ix^{(i)}x^{(i)\top}=U\Lambda U^\top,\quad \lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots
$$

Quadratic form in the eigenbasis, and explained variance of the top k components:

$$
u^\top\hat\Sigma u=\sum_{j}\lambda_j\alpha_j^2\le\lambda_1\ \ (\textstyle\sum_j\alpha_j^2=1),\qquad
\text{explained}=\frac{\sum_{j\le k}\lambda_j}{\operatorname{tr}(\hat\Sigma)}
$$

## Lecture 11: Diffusion models and their training objective

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI)

This lecture introduces diffusion models, the main approach to generating images (and increasingly video, robot actions and text) from a data distribution. A fixed forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise to a clean sample until it is pure noise, and a neural network is trained to reverse the process one small step at a time, so that generation starts from noise and denoises step by step. The lecturer derives the closed form of the forward process, explains why the reverse steps are Gaussian (with a short continuous time view), and then trains the model as a latent variable model using the ELBO from the EM lecture. A chain rule for KL divergence breaks the objective into one loss per step, each a squared difference between the network's predicted mean and a known Gaussian posterior mean. The lecture runs out of time before the final simplification.

### Key ideas

- [0:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=5s) Diffusion models are generative models mostly for images, but also used for video and robotics (many vision language action models use them to generate actions), and for text.
- [1:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=68s) The task: given many natural images, generate new clean realistic images from the same distribution, similar but not identical to the data.
- [1:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=101s) Before diffusion, GANs and variational autoencoders were the standard. Diffusion is now the main approach, so those topics are no longer taught.
- [2:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=133s) One advantage of diffusion for language is that it can generate in parallel, which can be faster than autoregressive models.
- [3:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=229s) Abstractly: we have samples x_0 from an unknown data distribution p_data in R^d and want a model that generates from it.
- [4:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=262s) Generation starts from white noise x_T, then a model p_theta applies steps to get x_T minus 1, x_T minus 2, and so on down to a clean x_0, a Markov chain. The image sharpens gradually.
- [6:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=365s) To learn this, simulate the opposite direction during training: a noising process q that adds Gaussian noise to clean images, then learn to reverse it.
- [7:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=464s) The forward step: x_t equals the square root of (1 minus beta_t) times x_{t-1}, plus the square root of beta_t times fresh standard Gaussian noise epsilon_t. Beta_t is a small number such as 0.01.
- [11:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=685s) These coefficients preserve scale: the covariance of x_t is (1 minus beta_t) times the covariance of x_{t-1} plus beta_t times the identity, so if data has identity covariance, every x_t has identity covariance.
- [14:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=842s) As t grows, the distribution of x_t converges to a standard Gaussian.
- [15:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=909s) Unrolling the recursion, with alpha_t equal to 1 minus beta_t, x_t becomes a constant times x_0 plus a linear combination of the epsilons.
- [16:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=1007s) With alpha bar_t as the product of alpha_1 through alpha_t, x_t equals the square root of alpha bar_t times x_0, plus the square root of (1 minus alpha bar_t) times a single standard Gaussian.
- [17:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=1072s) The noise coefficient has to be this value so that variance is preserved when x_0 has identity covariance.
- [19:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=1173s) The lecturer corrected a missing square root in the board derivation; the square roots appear because beta_t is the variance and its square root the standard deviation, a convention of the field.
- [23:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=1411s) Alpha bar_t goes to zero as t grows (a product of many numbers just below one), so x_T is approximately a standard Gaussian regardless of x_0.
- [27:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=1677s) The forward process is fixed. There is no training in it, because beta is chosen by design. In variational autoencoders the forward (encoder) process is learned too.
- [29:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=1774s) The true reverse process q(x_{t-1} given x_t) is unknown but it is also a Markov chain. We approximate it with a model.
- [30:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=1844s) Parameterization: p_theta(x_{t-1} given x_t) is a Gaussian whose mean mu_theta(x_t, t) is a neural network taking x_t and the time t, with a chosen variance sigma_t squared, not learned in this lecture.
- [33:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=2003s) Why not noise in one step? Adding noise gradually lets the reverse also be gradual, and each small denoising step is easier to learn. The original paper used T around a thousand steps, and faster samplers were found later.
- [34:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=2098s) A learned encoder with a few layers would be a variational autoencoder. Diffusion uses a coarse fixed noising with many steps instead.
- [36:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=2160s) Why Gaussian in reverse? In the continuous limit of tiny steps the true reverse process is Gaussian, and any approximation can still be trained with a Gaussian.
- [40:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=2447s) Why noise remains in the reverse: given x_t you cannot know exactly where it came from, since many paths lead there, so the reverse step is a distribution, not a point.
- [42:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=2545s) Gaussians arise because many small noise steps add up, by the law of large numbers, to a Gaussian.
- [44:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=2641s) Continuous view: the forward process is a stochastic differential equation with a drift term plus Brownian motion with variance beta.
- [45:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=2740s) A result going back to Anderson shows that the reversed time process is also a stochastic differential equation, with Brownian noise and a drift that also depends on the density.
- [50:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=3030s) Notation point: in machine learning, p(x_t | x_{t-1}) is used both for the density function and for values, and capital letters for random variables are usually not used.
- [55:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=3326s) Training is maximum likelihood of the data under p_theta, with the noisy images x_1 through x_T as hidden variables, so we use the ELBO from the EM lecture.
- [56:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=3393s) p_theta defines the joint distribution of x_0 through x_T and so also the marginal of x_0. Treat x_0 as the observed X and x_1 to x_T as the latent Z.
- [57:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=3461s) The ELBO form: expected reconstruction log likelihood under Q minus the KL divergence between Q and the prior over the latents.
- [58:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=3528s) The Q distribution is the forward process, the distribution of x_1 to x_T given x_0. Unlike a variational autoencoder, Q has no parameters.
- [65:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=3926s) The KL term acts as regularization: without it, the posterior could trivially copy x, giving perfect reconstruction and overfitting.
- [67:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=4024s) Chain rule for KL: the KL between two joint distributions equals the KL between the marginals plus the expected KL between the conditionals.
- [69:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=4191s) Applied to the Markov chains here, it splits the KL into a sum of terms, each involving only one step.
- [72:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=4359s) Each term compares the model's reverse step with the true reverse step given x_0, which is simple because given x_0 the forward process is known exactly.
- [75:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=4524s) The true reverse step given x_0, q(x_{t-1} given x_t, x_0), comes from Bayes' rule using the known Gaussian forward distributions.
- [77:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=4620s) It is a Gaussian whose mean is a linear combination of x_0 and x_t (called mu tilde) and whose variance depends only on alpha and beta.
- [80:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=4806s) Choosing sigma_t squared equal to that variance, the two Gaussians share a covariance, and their KL is just the squared difference of the means divided by twice the variance.
- [81:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqUMCzWjZSI&t=4907s) The loss is the squared difference between the network's predicted mean and mu tilde, which we minimize. A simplification follows next lecture.

### Formulas

Forward noising process (beta_t small, fresh Gaussian noise at each step), and why it preserves scale:

$$
x_t=\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\epsilon_t,\quad \epsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(x_t)=(1-\beta_t)\operatorname{Cov}(x_{t-1})+\beta_tI
$$

Closed form of the forward process, with alpha_t = 1 minus beta_t and the cumulative product alpha bar_t:

$$
\alpha_t=1-\beta_t,\quad \bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s,\qquad
x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\epsilon_t,\quad\hat\epsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
$$

The reverse process model (a Gaussian with a learned mean network):

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\mathcal{N}\big(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\,\sigma_t^2I\big)
$$

The forward process in continuous time (a drift plus Brownian motion), for small beta:

$$
dx_t=-\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x_t\,dt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dW_t
$$

ELBO with x_0 observed and x_1 through x_T latent, with Q the forward process:

$$
\log p_\theta(x_0)\ \ge\ \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\big[\log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_{1:T})\big]-\operatorname{KL}\big(q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)\,\big\|\,p_\theta(x_{1:T})\big)
$$

Chain rule for KL divergence:

$$
\operatorname{KL}\big(P_{XY}\,\|\,Q_{XY}\big)=\operatorname{KL}\big(P_Y\,\|\,Q_Y\big)+\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P}\Big[\operatorname{KL}\big(P_{X\mid y}\,\|\,Q_{X\mid y}\big)\Big]
$$

The true reverse step given x_0 is Gaussian (standard coefficients):

$$
q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)=\mathcal{N}\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0),\tilde\beta_tI\big),\quad
\tilde\mu_t=\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_t,\quad
\tilde\beta_t=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t
$$

The per step loss when sigma_t squared equals tilde beta_t (equal covariances), from the KL of two Gaussians:

$$
\operatorname{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2I)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(\tilde\mu,\sigma^2I)\big)=\frac{\|\mu-\tilde\mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2},\qquad
L_{t-1}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{2\tilde\beta_t}\big\|\mu_\theta(x_t,t)-\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)\big\|^2\Big]
$$

## Lecture 12: Noise prediction, foundation models and adaptation

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8). The YouTube title (Representation Learning) covers only the middle part; the first third finishes diffusion models and the last third covers fine tuning and LoRA

This lecture finishes diffusion models by turning the per step mean matching loss into the practical noise prediction objective, with the full training and sampling algorithms. It then starts the large language model block with the foundation model paradigm: pretraining on massive unlabeled data followed by adaptation to many tasks, with zero shot and few shot use. For adaptation that updates parameters it covers representation learning, linear probing, full fine tuning (and why initialization from a pretrained model matters even though many solutions have zero training loss), the LP FT recipe, and LoRA, which learns a low rank update to each weight matrix. The lecturer is candid about what LoRA does and does not save, and why serving many users from one shared base model is its main practical advantage.

### Key ideas

- [0:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=39s) The coming lectures cover how autoregressive language models (GPT) are trained, then reinforcement learning after training, with the basics of RL as needed, plus a guest lecture on systems and GPUs.
- [2:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=137s) Recap of diffusion: a fixed forward noising process q and a learned Gaussian reverse process p_theta, trained by maximizing an ELBO.
- [3:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=236s) The ELBO is a sum of KL terms over steps, each comparing the true reverse step given x_0 with the model's reverse step, plus a term for the final noise level and a reconstruction term.
- [5:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=345s) With matching variances, each KL term only compares the two means, as a squared difference.
- [7:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=441s) The term comparing the forward distribution at step T with the prior is zero in the limit, since both are standard Gaussians once enough noise has been added.
- [8:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=538s) The reconstruction term for x_0 given x_1 has the same form as the others, by defining mu tilde at the first step as x_0.
- [16:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=987s) mu_theta is the model's prediction of the mean of x_{t-1}, while mu tilde is what the true process would give if it knew x_0, a linear interpolation between x_0 and x_t.
- [18:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1090s) This is a fully implementable algorithm: sample x_t from the forward process, compute the target, apply the network, and minimize the loss by backpropagation.
- [20:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1223s) q(x_{t-1} given x_t, x_0) is Gaussian because, conditioned on x_0, the variables are jointly Gaussian, and conditionals of jointly Gaussian variables are Gaussian.
- [23:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1388s) Substituting x_0 in terms of x_t and the noise epsilon gives mu tilde as one over the square root of alpha_t times (x_t minus beta_t over the square root of (1 minus alpha bar_t) times epsilon).
- [24:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1461s) Since x_t is known, parameterize the network so that the known part is hardcoded and only the noise is predicted by the network.
- [25:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1527s) Subtracting the two means leaves a scalar coefficient times the difference between the true noise and the network's noise estimate.
- [26:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1568s) So the objective becomes noise prediction: guess the noise that was added. People then drop the scalar coefficients, leaving a very clean loss.
- [27:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1634s) Training: sample x_0 from the dataset, a step t uniformly from 1 to T, and noise epsilon; build x_t from x_0 and epsilon; minimize the squared difference between epsilon and the network's prediction.
- [29:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1770s) Sampling: draw x_T from a standard Gaussian, then for each step run the network, compute the mean, and add a bit of fresh noise to get x_{t-1}.
- [31:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1872s) The noise added at each reverse step uses the chosen standard deviation, sigma_t equal to the square root of beta tilde_t.
- [32:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=1940s) No strong theory proves why diffusion works well. One intuition: one direction of the process is fixed, and the long timeline makes every local denoising step easy to learn.
- [35:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2132s) Gradients come from backpropagation and the update from an optimizer such as SGD or Adam. New algorithms are often innovations at the loss level, while optimizer changes apply universally.
- [36:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2194s) For the rest of the course the focus is on data, architecture, loss function and learning procedure, assuming gradients can be computed.
- [37:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2226s) The term foundation model was coined by a Stanford report around 2021, after the excitement over GPT-3 in summer 2020, to describe an emerging new paradigm.
- [39:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2352s) The key words are pretraining, and now post training. There are two phases: pretraining and adaptation.
- [39:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2385s) Pretraining uses massive unlabeled data, often several orders of magnitude more than before, general purpose and diverse, with quality that mattered less at first.
- [41:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2479s) Adaptation then adapts the model to a wide range of tasks. In the past a model was trained for one task or a few; now the number of tasks is effectively unlimited.
- [44:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2641s) Few shot: you have a small labeled dataset for the new task (about 5 to 10 examples). Zero shot: you just describe the task and no new data is needed. Today zero shot is the main mode.
- [45:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2742s) Adaptation can update parameters or not. This lecture covers updating, and later lectures cover prompting without updates.
- [46:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2775s) Pretraining has a loss defined per data point and averaged, the same structure as before, but with no labels and a huge amount of data.
- [48:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=2881s) Representation learning is pretraining that produces a model f_theta mapping data to an m dimensional vector, called a representation, feature or embedding.
- [50:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3015s) Linear probing: freeze the pretrained representation and train only a linear head w on top, predicting with the dot product of w and the features (with a softmax for classification).
- [51:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3115s) This works much better than handcrafted features because the pretrained representation captures semantic information, so y becomes close to linear in the features.
- [53:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3212s) Models are increasingly multimodal, handling images, text, speech and video in one model.
- [55:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3309s) Fine tuning also optimizes theta, starting from the pretrained theta hat, with w initialized randomly.
- [56:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3403s) The loss is the same as for training from scratch, yet pretrained initialization gives much better test results. So the loss has many global minima with near zero training loss but different test performance.
- [60:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3600s) LP FT: first do linear probing to get w_0, then fine tune both from (w_0, theta hat). It is probably the dominant recipe.
- [61:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3695s) Why it helps is not fully understood, but a random head can distort the representation early in fine tuning, while a probed head changes it less.
- [62:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3769s) LoRA (low rank adaptation) is a technique to adapt a large model while changing few degrees of freedom.
- [63:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3832s) Motivation: the base model is huge and the new data small, so changing every parameter is unnecessary and invites overfitting.
- [64:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3895s) Reparameterize a weight matrix W as W plus A B, with A and B of inner rank r. A d by d matrix has d squared degrees of freedom, but the update has only 2 d r.
- [66:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=3960s) Freeze W_0 and train only A and B. Initialize A to zero and B randomly so the starting model equals the pretrained one.
- [68:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=4091s) Compute savings are small: the forward pass still computes W_0 x and the backward pass still multiplies by W_0 transposed. The weight gradient is a bit smaller.
- [69:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=4188s) Memory savings are real but modest: frozen weights stay in memory, but there are no gradients or optimizer states (such as Adam momentum) for W_0.
- [72:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=4325s) In sharded training (FSDP), gradients and optimizer states are already split across GPUs, so the saving is less dramatic.
- [73:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=4391s) LoRA matters most when memory is very limited, for example fitting a job on one GPU instead of two.
- [73:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=4425s) Its main advantage is serving: many users can share one base model W_0 with their own small low rank adapters, which is why fine tuning services from large providers are offered through LoRA.
- [75:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_kREM2UAiJ8&t=4522s) Switching between users means swapping small adapters, which are quick to load even from the CPU, so one deployment can serve very many users.

### Formulas

Diffusion ELBO written as one KL term per step, plus a prior term and a reconstruction term:

$$
\log p_\theta(x_0)\ \ge\ \mathbb{E}\big[\log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)\big]-\operatorname{KL}\big(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\|\,p(x_T)\big)-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\operatorname{KL}\big(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\big)\Big]
$$

The true reverse mean written in terms of the noise (eliminating x_0), and the matching parameterization of the network:

$$
\tilde\mu_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\hat\epsilon_t\Big),\qquad
\mu_\theta(x_t,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\Big)
$$

The noise prediction loss (coefficients dropped):

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x_0,\;t\sim\text{Unif}\{1..T\},\;\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\Big[\big\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\;t\big)\big\|^2\Big]
$$

One reverse sampling step, with z standard Gaussian and sigma_t equal to the square root of beta tilde_t:

$$
x_{t-1}=\mu_\theta(x_t,t)+\sigma_t\,z,\qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
$$

Linear probing and full fine tuning (the pretrained parameters are theta hat, f is the representation):

$$
\hat w=\arg\min_{w}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}L\big(w^\top f_{\hat\theta}(x^{(i)}),y^{(i)}\big),\qquad
(\hat w,\hat\theta')=\arg\min_{w,\theta}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}L\big(w^\top f_\theta(x^{(i)}),y^{(i)}\big)\ \text{ starting from }\theta=\hat\theta
$$

LoRA: freeze W_0 and learn a rank r update (the parameter count drops from d squared to 2dr):

$$
W=W_0+AB,\qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d},\ \ r\ll d,\qquad A_{\text{init}}=0
$$

## Lecture 13: Contrastive embeddings, semantic search and RAG

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4). The YouTube title mentions LLMs and the next word prediction loss, but that does not match the lecture: the transcript covers contrastive representation learning, semantic search and retrieval augmented generation, and does not cover next word prediction

This lecture shows how embeddings are trained and used. It first contrasts supervised pretraining (the penultimate layer of a classifier) with contrastive learning, which needs no labels: two augmentations of one image should have close embeddings while embeddings of different images should be far apart, which is turned into a softmax cross entropy loss over a batch similarity matrix. The lecturer discusses negative pairs, hard negatives and how to build batches, and notes that the same idea builds shared embeddings for text and images. The second half applies embeddings to semantic search (precompute document vectors, embed the query, find the largest dot product) and to retrieval augmented generation, where retrieved documents are placed in a language model's context instead of retraining it.

### Key ideas

- [0:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=38s) An embedding model maps each input (image, text, audio, video) to a vector. The goal is that similar inputs get nearby vectors and different inputs get faraway ones.
- [2:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=133s) Embeddings enable similarity search: embed a cat image and find the images whose embeddings are closest. The same applies to text, audio and video.
- [2:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=168s) Linear probing on embeddings still exists but is less used now, since you can train an end to end model. The most important use of embeddings today is similarity search.
- [3:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=232s) Supervised pretraining: train a large classifier on labeled data (for example the thousand ImageNet classes) and use the penultimate layer as the representation, discarding the rest.
- [5:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=329s) The labels must be numerous and diverse. With only binary labels, or only digits 0 to 9, the network can solve the task with simple features that do not transfer.
- [6:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=393s) Scaling supervised pretraining means collecting ever more labels, which is expensive. That motivates contrastive learning, which learns without image labels.
- [7:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=460s) Augmentation: create two random versions of an image with random crops, rotations, blur, noise or color changes. Random cropping is probably the most important.
- [13:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=811s) Contrastive idea: pass both augmentations of the same image through the network and make their embeddings close.
- [14:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=844s) Closeness alone would collapse everything to one point, so we also need a push: embeddings of augmentations of different images must be far apart.
- [15:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=941s) Problem: two different images may be similar (two cats). Pushing them apart is counterproductive, but most random pairs really are different, so the benefit outweighs the harm.
- [20:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=1204s) The goal is not random placement. Similar items should cluster tightly (even cats of the same breed first), with hierarchy a secondary effect.
- [23:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=1396s) The loss does some damage on look alike pairs, but other terms help, so it does not succeed in pushing real cats apart.
- [24:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=1493s) Unlabeled diverse data is easy to get (just download images), whereas labels are expensive.
- [26:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=1596s) SimCLR style loss: take a batch of B images, make two augmentations of each, giving 2B examples x hat i and x tilde i.
- [27:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=1661s) Build a B by B matrix of similarities between x hat i and x tilde j. The diagonal (same image) should be large and the off diagonal entries small.
- [28:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=1725s) Similarity is the dot product of embeddings, usually normalized, which makes it the cosine similarity.
- [30:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=1822s) How large is large? For each row, the loss is minus the log of the exponential of the diagonal similarity divided by the sum of exponentials of all similarities in that row.
- [34:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2052s) The loss decreases when the diagonal entry grows and when off diagonal entries shrink, and what matters is the diagonal being much larger than the others.
- [34:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2084s) It is a classification: given x hat i, identify which x tilde is its partner among all the others. This is a softmax cross entropy over similarity logits, summed over all i.
- [37:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2250s) Monotonicity check with a as the positive term and b as the sum of negatives: the loss minus log of a over (a plus b) falls as a rises and as b falls.
- [39:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2364s) A matching pair is a positive pair, and every other pair is a negative pair, though some negatives may actually be similar.
- [39:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2396s) Positive pairs need not come from augmentation: pair an image with its text description, which builds a shared embedding space for text and images.
- [40:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2459s) There are far more negative pairs than positive pairs, which is fine because each negative gives a weak signal.
- [41:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2490s) In practice people need stronger negatives than random ones. Hard negative mining looks for pairs that look similar but are not the same.
- [42:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2554s) Example of a hard negative: a photo of a child playing soccer and text about the FIFA World Cup. Hard negatives make the loss more demanding, but if they are too similar they should be positives.
- [44:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2655s) The B squared similarity computations are cheap. The bottleneck is running 2B forward passes through a huge network to get the embeddings.
- [45:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2750s) B is a batch of 128 up to several thousand examples, not the whole dataset, and fresh random augmentations are generated each time through the data.
- [46:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2813s) Batches are often drawn from a single source to make negatives harder. For code, take examples from one programming language or one repository.
- [48:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2911s) For language there are often no augmentations. Positive pairs can be the title and sections of a document paired with its raw text, with other documents as negatives.
- [49:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=2975s) Semantic search: given a query and a collection of documents or images, find the most relevant ones.
- [50:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=3006s) Precompute embeddings of all documents once. At query time, embed the query and find the document embedding with the largest dot product.
- [51:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=3070s) Brute force just takes dot products against the stored vectors, without rerunning the network. Vector databases and k nearest neighbor search algorithms speed this up.
- [53:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=3208s) Retrieval augmented generation (RAG) feeds a language model private or fresh information at inference time, without retraining it.
- [54:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=3271s) Alternatives like fine tuning on your own data can be too expensive, and you may not want to host a huge model.
- [55:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=3334s) RAG: search your corpus for the relevant documents, put a handful (often 5 to 10) in the model's context, and the model sees the query and documents together to generate the answer.
- [57:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=3428s) Advantages: it is modular and cheap, access control is easy (users cannot retrieve documents they may not see), and deleting a document is easy.
- [58:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=3492s) Forgetting information a model was trained on is an open research problem, while removing a document from the corpus is trivial.
- [58:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=3524s) Some systems use regular expression or keyword search (generated by language models) instead of semantic search, which works well for structured data like code.
- [59:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNTajqxxOn4&t=3593s) Semantic search remains useful where keyword matching is hard, and the best retrieval method still depends on the task, perhaps combining both.

### Formulas

Similarity between two normalized embeddings (the dot product equals the cosine similarity), and the batch similarity matrix between the two augmentations of each image:

$$
s(x,z)=\varphi(x)^\top\varphi(z),\ \ \|\varphi(\cdot)\|=1,\qquad
S_{ij}=\varphi(\hat x_i)^\top\varphi(\tilde x_j),\quad i,j=1,\dots,B
$$

Contrastive (SimCLR style) loss for a batch: a softmax cross entropy over each row, with the diagonal entry as the correct class:

$$
\ell_i=-\log\frac{\exp(S_{ii})}{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\exp(S_{ij})},\qquad
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{B}\ell_i
$$

Semantic search by maximum inner product (document embeddings computed in advance):

$$
i^\star=\arg\max_{i}\;\varphi(q)^\top\varphi(d_i)
$$

## Lecture 14: Autoregressive language models and transformer attention

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI). The YouTube title also mentions in context learning, but the transcript does not discuss it

This lecture opens the large language model block by defining an autoregressive language model: text is split into subword tokens, the joint probability of a token sequence is factored by the chain rule into next token conditionals, and each conditional is a softmax over the vocabulary produced by a transformer. The lecture covers tokenization (why subwords, vocabularies of roughly 100 thousand to 250 thousand tokens, BPE), embeddings, autoregressive generation with temperature and top k sampling, and the training loss, which is the negative log likelihood summed over positions. It then builds the transformer: alternating attention and MLP layers, single head attention with queries, keys and values, causal masking, multiple heads, residual connections and normalization. It closes with the quadratic cost of attention in sequence length and flash attention for memory.

### Key ideas

- [0:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=6s) Large language models are the next topic, with only a few lectures, interleaved with the basics of reinforcement learning needed to discuss reasoning models. There will be a guest lecture on systems for machine learning.
- [0:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=37s) The new paradigm began with GPT-3, autoregressive language models, and these are the focus (early non autoregressive transformers are skipped).
- [1:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=69s) Autoregressive means generating tokens one after another, each depending on the previous ones, so the distribution of language is modeled sequentially.
- [2:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=134s) Transformers work on numbers, so text must be tokenized: split into the smallest units fed to the model. Characters give too many steps, and whole words handle rare long words badly.
- [2:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=165s) A rare word such as internationalization would be treated as unrelated to internationalize or nation if it were a single token, so subword tokenization is preferred.
- [3:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=229s) A new word can be split into known pieces, so understanding its parts gives understanding of the whole. This is common in biology and in German.
- [4:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=292s) A tokenizer has a fixed dictionary of subwords. Almost all common words are tokens, and a leading space can be part of a token.
- [5:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=356s) Open source models have vocabularies around 100 thousand to 250 thousand tokens.
- [6:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=391s) Tokenizing text gives a sequence of token IDs. Each model has its own tokenizer, and the dictionary depends on the model.
- [9:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=553s) BPE (byte pair encoding) builds the vocabulary with a greedy algorithm that finds frequent subwords.
- [10:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=616s) From now on, text means a sequence of tokens, each an index into a dictionary V, with about 250 thousand entries.
- [11:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=680s) The number of possible sequences of length T is V to the T, too many to give each its own probability.
- [11:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=712s) The chain rule factors the joint probability into a product of next token conditionals: P(x1) times P(x2 given x1), and so on up to P(x_T given x_1 to x_{T-1}).
- [12:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=745s) Each conditional is a distribution over only V options, so a softmax over V logits is enough. A transformer models every conditional.
- [14:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=878s) Pipeline: token IDs are converted into learned embeddings, which are rows of a V by D embedding matrix, then passed through a transformer to output logits at each position.
- [16:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=1004s) A beginning of sentence token x_0 is often added at the start, one of the special tokens reserved in the dictionary.
- [17:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=1074s) The output at position t gives the logits for the next token, and softmax of these logits is the distribution over the V tokens.
- [24:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=1455s) The model is causal: the output at a position depends only on earlier inputs. Embeddings depend only on the token itself.
- [25:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=1552s) With this probabilistic model, learning is maximum likelihood on any data sequence.
- [26:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=1584s) Generation: sample x_1 from the softmax, feed it back, sample x_2, and so on. This is why it is called autoregressive.
- [28:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=1686s) Often generation begins from a prompt: given the first k tokens, the model continues from token k plus 1.
- [29:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=1749s) Temperature divides the logits by tau before the softmax. It does not change the order of the logits, only the sharpness of the distribution.
- [30:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=1845s) Example: logits 2, 1, minus 1 give probabilities about 0.71, 0.26 and 0.04. Dividing by a tiny tau makes them nearly 1, 0, 0, so sampling is almost deterministic.
- [34:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2053s) A low temperature gives more deterministic generation and zero temperature always picks the most likely token, while a temperature above one flattens the distribution to produce more diversity, which is useful in reinforcement learning.
- [35:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2118s) Top k sampling keeps only the k most likely tokens and renormalizes, avoiding rare tokens.
- [36:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2184s) Training: the negative log likelihood of a sequence is minus the sum over positions of the log of the probability the model gives to the actual next token.
- [37:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2258s) In practice you read the entry of the softmax at the true token's index, take the log, sum over positions, and optimize with SGD or Adam.
- [39:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2353s) A transformer alternates attention layers and MLPs. Attention maps a sequence of vectors to a sequence of vectors, and an MLP is applied to each position's vector.
- [40:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2452s) The MLP is a two or three layer network with shared parameters and is applied independently at each position. Attention is the only place positions interact.
- [41:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2517s) The whole system is trained end to end as one giant network with automatic differentiation.
- [43:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2582s) Single head attention maps input vectors h_1 to h_T to output vectors of the same count.
- [44:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2647s) Each input vector is multiplied by learned matrices to get a query, a key and a value, row vectors of dimension d_h.
- [46:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2775s) Intuition: to process one position, figure out which other positions hold relevant information, pay attention to them, and compute an output from them.
- [47:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2873s) To decide where position t looks, take the dot product of its query with every key. A scaling factor is usually included.
- [49:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=2978s) A softmax over these T scores gives a probability vector p_t over positions, with larger entries where the query and key align.
- [51:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=3077s) The output at position t is the sum over positions of p_ti times the value v_i, a weighted average of the values.
- [53:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=3239s) Without attention, an MLP applied per token has no interaction between positions and cannot understand sentences.
- [55:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=3302s) A giant MLP over the concatenated sequence would need too many parameters (sequence length can reach millions) and its size would change with the sequence length.
- [56:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=3396s) Attention is a compromise between structure and flexibility. Convolutional networks also work to some extent but not as well.
- [58:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=3494s) In matrix form, the queries, keys and values are T by d_h matrices, and the output is row wise softmax of QK transpose over a scale, times V.
- [60:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=3623s) As written, output t depends on later inputs too, which breaks the autoregressive property.
- [63:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=3787s) Fix with a causal mask: add minus infinity to the entries above the diagonal of the score matrix, so that after softmax those weights are zero and the rest renormalize.
- [65:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=3919s) Masking is needed because the model cannot see future tokens when generating the current token.
- [66:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=3985s) Multi head attention: run several single head attentions in parallel on the same input, each with its own W_Q, W_K, W_V.
- [68:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=4083s) Concatenate the heads' outputs at each position and multiply by another projection matrix to get the final output.
- [69:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=4183s) Intuitively different heads can attend to different kinds of information, such as entities or sentiment. Large models can have on the order of 100 heads.
- [72:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=4346s) In a full block, attention and the MLP are wrapped with normalization (often RMSNorm) and residual connections. Pre norm and post norm place the normalization differently.
- [74:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=4442s) Attention costs order T squared times d_h, since each of T positions takes T dot products, which is the key limit for long context.
- [75:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=4506s) At a million tokens, T squared is a trillion operations, which is why long contexts get compressed in practice. Real systems use variants to reduce this toward linear.
- [76:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=4570s) The T by T score matrix does not need to be stored in full: flash attention processes blocks of it against blocks of V to reduce memory.
- [77:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pwQ0l4hFCVI&t=4635s) Architectures that improve the T dependence lose some expressiveness and may lose performance.

### Formulas

Chain rule factorization of a token sequence, and each conditional as a softmax of the transformer's logits u_t:

$$
p_\theta(x_1,\dots,x_T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta\big(x_t\mid x_1,\dots,x_{t-1}\big),\qquad
p_\theta\big(x_t\mid x_{<t}\big)=\operatorname{softmax}\big(u_t\big),\quad u_t=f_\theta(x_0,\dots,x_{t-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
$$

Training loss, the negative log likelihood (the subscript picks the entry of the softmax at the true token):

$$
-\log p_\theta(x_1,\dots,x_T)=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\Big[\operatorname{softmax}\big(f_\theta(x_0,\dots,x_{t-1})\big)\Big]_{x_t}
$$

Sampling with temperature tau (a lower tau sharpens the distribution):

$$
p_\tau(x_t=j\mid x_{<t})=\frac{\exp(u_{t,j}/\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{V}\exp(u_{t,k}/\tau)}
$$

Single head attention for row vectors h_i (W matrices are d by d_h, c is a scale such as the square root of d_h):

$$
q_t=h_tW_Q,\ \ k_t=h_tW_K,\ \ v_t=h_tW_V,\qquad
p_{ti}=\frac{\exp\big(q_tk_i^\top/c\big)}{\sum_{j}\exp\big(q_tk_j^\top/c\big)},\qquad
h_t^{\text{out}}=\sum_{i}p_{ti}\,v_i
$$

Attention in matrix form with a causal mask M (zero on and below the diagonal, minus infinity above):

$$
\operatorname{Attn}(H)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{c}+M\Big)V,\qquad M_{ij}=\begin{cases}0 & j\le i\\ -\infty & j>i\end{cases}
$$

Multi head attention: concatenate the heads and project:

$$
h_t^{\text{out}}=\big[\,h_t^{(1)}\ \big|\ h_t^{(2)}\ \big|\ \cdots\ \big|\ h_t^{(n_h)}\,\big]\,W_O
$$

## Lecture 15: KV cache, mixture of experts, in context learning and SFT

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg) (the YouTube title calls it Lecture 16). The YouTube title (basic concepts in RL and policy gradient) does not match the transcript, which covers attention variants, mixture of experts, in context learning and instruction tuning, and does not cover reinforcement learning

This lecture continues with practical transformer topics. It starts with the KV cache and why it limits inference memory and batch size, then covers attention variants that reduce cost: grouped query attention (fewer key and value sets than query heads), sliding window attention (cost T times a window W instead of T squared), the square root scaling of scores, and normalizing queries and keys. It then explains mixture of experts, where a router sends each token at each layer to a few of many expert MLPs, giving a large total parameter count but a small active one, with the training and serving challenges that brings. The last third covers using a pretrained model without updating weights (few shot in context learning and zero shot prompting) and strengthening those abilities with supervised instruction tuning.

### Key ideas

- [0:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=39s) Plan: variations of attention that improve efficiency, mixture of experts, then ways to use large language models (few shot, zero shot, supervised fine tuning).
- [1:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=103s) Review: each head has queries, keys and values for every time step. A causal mask means each query only attends to the past, so only the lower triangle of the score matrix is used.
- [4:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=298s) Attention compute grows as T squared, while the memory to store keys and values grows linearly with T.
- [5:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=330s) Only keys and values need to be stored during generation, since a query is used only at its own step. This stored data is the KV cache, for every head and layer.
- [6:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=362s) The KV cache takes a huge amount of GPU memory, limiting how many sequences can be batched together during generation.
- [6:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=394s) A small batch means low arithmetic intensity: the GPU waits on memory bandwidth instead of computing, so utilization (MFU) is low.
- [7:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=464s) Grouped query attention keeps all the query heads but shares keys and values across groups of heads, so there are fewer key and value sequences to store.
- [9:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=562s) With N_H query heads and N_G key groups, each key and value set serves tau equals N_H over N_G query heads. The saving is memory, not compute.
- [13:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=832s) Query head j uses key group g(j), computed from (j minus 1) divided by tau, rounded down, plus 1. The rest of attention is unchanged.
- [16:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=969s) Scores are divided by a constant c, commonly the square root of d_h, because the dot product of two random d_h dimensional vectors with entries near plus or minus one has size about the square root of d_h.
- [17:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=1034s) This scaling keeps the softmax input at unit scale whatever d_h is, but it is a heuristic with no strict theory, and other normalizers may be justified.
- [18:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=1097s) Why share keys and values rather than queries: during generation only the latest query is needed, while keys and values for the entire past must be stored.
- [21:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=1272s) If tau equals N_H there is a single key and value set (multi query attention), the lowest memory use but possibly too little stored information. People moved to a moderate number of groups.
- [23:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=1436s) The grouping must be regular and fixed so it can be compiled to run fast on GPUs, and key sets can be placed on the same device as their queries.
- [26:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=1591s) Sliding window attention avoids T squared compute: each query attends only to the last W positions, so the cost is T times W, linear in T.
- [28:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=1724s) With L layers the effective look back is at most L times W tokens, since lower layers see further back. Beyond that the model forgets the past.
- [29:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=1790s) Sliding windows alone forget long context. Other sparse attention methods exist, and it is not known which is best.
- [30:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=1857s) Normalizing queries and keys (for example with RMSNorm) before the dot product keeps score magnitudes stable, a heuristic used in some open models.
- [32:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=1961s) The MLP part of a transformer is also called a feed forward network. Making it wider increases computation and memory.
- [34:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2059s) Mixture of experts: instead of one big MLP, have many expert MLPs and a router that sends each input vector to only a few of them, averaging their outputs.
- [35:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2130s) This separates memory from computation: many parameters stored, but only a few experts active per token.
- [36:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2196s) Models are named like 30B A3B: 30 billion total parameters and 3 billion active per token.
- [37:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2228s) The original motivation was specialization: experts for math, physics, plain English or code, so smaller specialized networks match the quality.
- [38:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2325s) Routing is decided per token and per layer, not per sequence. Specialization emerges during training and is not clearly interpretable.
- [39:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2389s) Explicitly training experts separately does not work as well as joint training, and with hundreds of experts it is hard to organize.
- [42:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2563s) The router projects the hidden vector with a routing matrix to an M dimensional score vector (through a softmax or sigmoid), and the top k scores pick the active experts.
- [44:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2668s) The output is a weighted sum of the selected experts' outputs, with weights equal to the router scores renormalized to sum to one over the chosen set.
- [46:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2770s) Some designs add a shared expert that is always used (as in DeepSeek V3), for general knowledge, with the rest specialized.
- [47:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2833s) Training adds regularization so routing is spread evenly over experts. If routing collapses to a few experts, the memory for the others is wasted.
- [48:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2897s) Uneven routing also makes the infrastructure harder to use efficiently, since some devices are idle. Providers can allocate more GPUs to busy experts.
- [49:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=2969s) The benefit is efficiency: many total parameters with controlled compute. All experts must still be in memory, but only the chosen ones compute.
- [50:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3033s) A dense trillion parameter model would be too slow and costly to serve, whereas a trillion parameter mixture with 30 billion active acts like a 30 billion model for compute.
- [52:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3131s) In context learning: put a few input and output examples in the prompt as a formatted string, add the test input, and let the model complete the output.
- [53:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3230s) Example: pairs like 2 and 3 giving 5, and 6 and 7 giving 13, then 15 and 2: the model can infer the symbol means plus and answer 17.
- [55:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3325s) A classification task can be given the same way, with an unexplained label set and example messages, and the model infers what the labels mean from the examples.
- [58:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3488s) Crucially, no parameters are updated. The examples are just context, so in context learning picks up styles and simple definitions, not new fundamental capabilities.
- [58:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3522s) Harder tasks, such as classifying biomedical images, are not solved by a few examples and would still need adaptation.
- [59:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3585s) Zero shot: just describe the task, as precisely as possible and possibly the output format (such as JSON), with no examples. This is how people mostly use chat models.
- [60:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3653s) The GPT-3 paper's key discovery was that few shot and zero shot work without any parameter update, which was shocking because new tasks were assumed to require retraining.
- [61:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3685s) This changed deployment: instead of collecting data and training a separate model per company, everyone uses one model with their own prompts, or even an onboarding document describing a workflow.
- [62:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3779s) These abilities emerged with scale and data, and can also appear in smaller models distilled from large ones, partly because training data now contains such examples.
- [64:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3874s) To strengthen these abilities, people use supervised fine tuning on instruction and response pairs, called instruction tuning.
- [66:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=3972s) The loss is the negative log likelihood of the response y given the instruction x. The instruction is given, and only the response tokens are predicted.
- [66:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=4005s) It decomposes over response tokens: at each step, predict the next token given the instruction and the response so far, averaged over examples.
- [67:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=4068s) Supervised fine tuning means supervision from the label y plus starting from a pretrained checkpoint. Instruction tuning is SFT on an instruction dataset.
- [68:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=4133s) Instructions define the task and sometimes the format, not the method for solving it.
- [69:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=4198s) A chain of thought written by people may constrain the model to a method it would not choose. Ideally it is generated by the model itself, and reinforcement learning is expected to do better than SFT here.
- [71:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=4297s) A chat model has already been instruction tuned, using collected instruction and response pairs, before you ever use it. Later stages use reinforcement learning.
- [72:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hHC-SF3utxg&t=4360s) With little data, fine tuning all experts with low rank methods is preferred to tuning a subset.

### Formulas

KV cache memory for one sequence (L layers, n_kv key and value sets per layer, head dimension d_h, T tokens, b bytes per number; the 2 is for keys and values):

$$
\text{KV cache}=2\cdot L\cdot n_{kv}\cdot d_h\cdot T\cdot b\ \text{ bytes}
$$

Grouped query attention: N_H query heads share N_G key and value sets, with tau equal to N_H over N_G and a group map g:

$$
g(j)=\Big\lfloor\frac{j-1}{\tau}\Big\rfloor+1,\qquad
h_j^{\text{out}}=\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{Q_{j}K_{g(j)}^\top}{c}+M\Big)V_{g(j)},\qquad c=\sqrt{d_h}
$$

Sliding window attention of width W (the mask keeps only the last W keys), with its cost and effective look back for L layers:

$$
\text{cost}=O(T\,W),\qquad \text{look back}\le L\,W
$$

Query key normalization before the dot product:

$$
\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{\operatorname{RMSNorm}(Q)\,\operatorname{RMSNorm}(K)^\top}{c}+M\Big)V
$$

Mixture of experts layer: router scores over M experts, the top k set S, and the renormalized weighted output:

$$
r=\sigma(hW_r)\in\mathbb{R}^{M},\quad S=\text{indices of the }k\text{ largest }r_s,\quad
\alpha_s=\frac{r_s}{\sum_{s'\in S}r_{s'}},\quad
\text{out}=\sum_{s\in S}\alpha_s\,E_s(h)
$$

Supervised instruction tuning loss over n instruction and response pairs (only response tokens are predicted):

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}\log p_\theta\big(y^{(i)}_t\mid x^{(i)},\,y^{(i)}_{<t}\big)
$$

## Lecture 16: Reinforcement learning basics: MDPs and policy gradient

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw) (the YouTube title calls it Lecture 18). The YouTube title (GMM EM, PCA) is wrong: the lecture is an introduction to reinforcement learning

This lecture starts reinforcement learning, using a robot on a one dimensional strip as the running example. It frames RL as sequential decision making with reward instead of labels, and defines a Markov decision process: states, actions, transition probabilities, a reward function and a discount factor. The Markov property implies that the best action depends only on the current state, which gives the idea of a policy, and the lecture defines value functions and derives the Bellman equation as a recursion that turns into a linear system. The second half introduces the policy gradient algorithm (REINFORCE), the ancestor of the methods used to train language models, and uses the log derivative trick to get a gradient that can be estimated from sampled trajectories without knowing the environment dynamics. The derivation stops just before the variance discussion.

### Key ideas

- [0:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=5s) Reinforcement learning starts here. Today covers the basics with robotics as the target example, and next week applies RL to large language models and long chains of reasoning.
- [0:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=38s) The lecture introduces the policy gradient algorithm, one of the fundamental algorithms for both robotics and large language models.
- [1:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=78s) RL addresses sequential decision making: decisions that have consequences in the future, taken over many steps, so a greedy choice may be suboptimal.
- [2:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=173s) A second aspect is risk versus return: some decisions gather information instead of maximizing the immediate payoff. This is the trade off between exploration and exploitation.
- [3:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=236s) Exploration is subtle in many modern applications and is often left to the algorithm's built in randomness, so it is not discussed much here.
- [4:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=299s) Most RL has no supervision of the classification type. You do not know the optimal actions. You only get a reward, a scalar that says whether a sequence of actions was good or bad.
- [6:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=372s) The agent collects its own data by trial and error, then reinforces good actions and discourages bad ones, a cycle of data collection and learning.
- [7:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=468s) The modeling framework is the Markov decision process (MDP), illustrated with a robot on a strip of ten cells that must reach a goal.
- [8:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=534s) States S describe the world (the robot's cell; for a real robot, joint angles and camera images; for Go, the whole board). What to include in the state is a modeling choice.
- [11:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=662s) Actions A are the controls available, such as moving left or right, or the force applied at each joint.
- [12:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=732s) Transition dynamics P_sa say what happens when you apply action a in state s: a probability distribution over the next state.
- [14:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=868s) Example: telling the robot at cell 7 to go left gives probability 0.9 of reaching cell 6 and 0.1 of staying at 7, and zero for all other cells. For each s and a the probabilities sum to one.
- [16:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1000s) The interaction loop: the agent chooses a_0 in s_0, the next state s_1 is sampled from P_{s_0 a_0}, the agent chooses a_1, and so on.
- [18:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1099s) The reward function R maps a state to a real number (often between minus 1 and 1) saying how good that state is. It can also depend on the action and the next state.
- [20:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1200s) To encode a goal at cell 9, give reward 1 at the goal and a small negative reward elsewhere, which pushes the robot to reach it quickly and stay.
- [22:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1328s) Reward shaping makes the reward gradually better near the goal so learning is easier, even if you only care about the goal itself.
- [22:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1362s) Shaping can backfire: a hidden teleport tunnel, or an obstacle in the way, can make the closer is better reward misleading, so with little knowledge of the environment it is safer to reward only the final outcome.
- [25:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1533s) A trajectory is a sequence of states and actions. The payoff of a trajectory is the sum of rewards, and we care about its expectation, since states are random.
- [26:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1603s) A discount factor gamma between 0 and 1, such as 0.99, multiplies the reward at step t by gamma to the t. It is like an interest rate that makes earlier rewards worth more.
- [28:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1701s) With rewards bounded by M, the discounted sum is bounded by M over (1 minus gamma), even for an infinite number of steps. Discounting also encourages reaching the goal sooner.
- [29:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1769s) An MDP is the combination of states, actions, transition dynamics, reward function and discount factor.
- [32:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1936s) The goal is to maximize the expected discounted payoff by choosing actions well.
- [33:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=1998s) Markov property: the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on history. So the future is independent of the past given the present.
- [34:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=2071s) Hence the optimal action depends only on the current state, and we look for a policy, a map from states to actions.
- [35:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=2137s) There is always an optimal deterministic policy, because you can pick the best action, and any one if several tie.
- [36:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=2200s) Randomized policies pi(a given s) are also used. They are no better than the best deterministic one, but they give continuity during training, since a distribution can change smoothly while a hard choice switches abruptly.
- [40:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=2436s) The value function V pi of s is the expected discounted return when starting in state s and following policy pi.
- [41:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=2513s) V star of s is the maximum over policies of V pi of s, and the optimal policy is the one achieving it.
- [43:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=2609s) The Bellman equation turns the sequential problem into a recursion. It matters more for classic RL than for RL on language models.
- [44:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=2675s) Warm up: the sum 1 plus gamma plus gamma squared and so on equals V, and factoring out gamma gives V equals 1 plus gamma V, so V equals 1 over (1 minus gamma).
- [46:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=2771s) Applying the same idea to V pi: the first reward is R(s), and the remaining discounted terms are gamma times the expected value from the random next state.
- [50:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=3002s) So V pi of s equals R(s) plus gamma times the sum over next states of the transition probability times V pi of the next state, a recursion with V pi on both sides.
- [51:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=3098s) For a finite number of states, these equations form a linear system in the unknowns V pi of s, which a standard linear solver can solve directly.
- [56:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=3378s) Policy gradient, also called REINFORCE, is the ancestor of the algorithms used to train language models. It works only with stochastic policies.
- [57:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=3474s) The policy is a neural network pi_theta(a given s). Two requirements: it can output the density (or probability) of any action given the state, and you can sample actions from it.
- [59:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=3546s) The objective is the expected total discounted reward under the policy, with the initial state drawn from a distribution, and the states following the transition dynamics.
- [60:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=3643s) The plan is to take the gradient of this expected reward and do gradient ascent on theta.
- [61:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=3680s) The difficulty: theta does not appear inside the expectation. It only affects the distribution of the trajectories being sampled, so the gradient cannot simply be moved inside.
- [64:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=3881s) Write the expectation as an integral of probability times value. The gradient can be moved inside the integral and acts only on the probability p_theta(x).
- [66:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=3982s) Log derivative trick: the gradient of p_theta equals p_theta times the gradient of log p_theta. This brings back an expectation over x drawn from p_theta.
- [68:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=4120s) The resulting gradient is an expectation, so it can be estimated by sampling. Note it is an estimator of the gradient, not of the original function.
- [69:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=4187s) For trajectories, the log probability is a sum of log initial state, log policy and log transition terms.
- [72:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=4363s) The transition terms do not depend on theta, so their gradient is zero. You do not need to know the environment dynamics, only the policy's log probabilities.
- [74:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xveNBYVTrqw&t=4480s) You never enumerate all trajectories. You sample one trajectory (or a handful) and use the sample as the estimate. The variance is not astronomical, though reducing it is the next topic.

### Formulas

Transition dynamics are a probability distribution over next states, and the discounted return is bounded when rewards are bounded by M:

$$
\sum_{s'}P_{sa}(s')=1,\qquad
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^tR(s_t)\ \le\ \frac{M}{1-\gamma}
$$

Value function of a policy, optimal value and optimal policy:

$$
V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^tR(s_t)\ \Big|\ s_0=s,\ \pi\Big],\qquad
V^\star(s)=\max_\pi V^\pi(s),\qquad \pi^\star=\arg\max_\pi V^\pi
$$

Geometric series by recursion, and the Bellman equation for a policy (which is a linear system):

$$
V=1+\gamma V\Rightarrow V=\frac{1}{1-\gamma},\qquad
V^\pi(s)=R(s)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P_{s\pi(s)}(s')\,V^\pi(s')\ \Longleftrightarrow\ V^\pi=(I-\gamma P_\pi)^{-1}R
$$

The policy gradient objective for a parameterized stochastic policy:

$$
\eta(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big],\qquad s_0\sim D_0,\quad a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t),\quad s_{t+1}\sim P_{s_ta_t}
$$

Log derivative (score function) trick:

$$
\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[f(x)\big]=\int f(x)\,\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)\,dx=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[f(x)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\big]
$$

Applied to a trajectory (the transition terms do not depend on theta, so they drop out), which gives the REINFORCE gradient:

$$
\log p_\theta(\tau)=\log D_0(s_0)+\sum_{t}\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)+\sum_{t}\log P_{s_ta_t}(s_{t+1}),\qquad
\nabla_\theta\eta=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big)\Big(\sum_{t}\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big)\Big]
$$

## Lecture 17: Policy gradient refinements, PPO and RL for reasoning

> **Watch:** [CS229 Lecture 17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg) (the YouTube title calls it Lecture 20). The YouTube title (GMM EM, PCA) is wrong: this last lecture finishes policy gradient, introduces PPO and applies reinforcement learning to language model reasoning

This last lecture finishes policy gradient and then connects it to how reasoning language models are trained. It shows that the expected gradient of the log probability of an action is zero, which lets you drop past rewards (reward to go) and subtract a state dependent baseline to reduce variance without changing the expectation. Because policy gradient is on policy and cannot reuse samples, PPO reuses old samples through importance sampling and clips the probability ratio to keep updates small; the lecturer explains the four clipping cases and is frank that there is no convincing theory for why it works. The final third treats a language model as an MDP whose actions are tokens and whose reward comes only from checking the final answer, explains why RL beats supervised fine tuning for long reasoning, and describes the group baseline of several samples per question and the practical requirements (a nonzero starting success rate, careful rewards, slow generation).

### Key ideas

- [0:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=6s) This is the last lecture. It finishes policy gradient, introduces PPO (a policy gradient extension that is among the most popular algorithms for training large language models), and then covers RL for long chains of reasoning with thinking tokens.
- [1:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=103s) Recall the problem: the parameters appear in the sampling distribution rather than inside the expectation, so the gradient cannot simply be moved inside. The policy gradient estimator solves this.
- [2:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=169s) Intuition: policy gradient maximizes the log probability of the actions taken, but weighted by reward. High reward trajectories get a large weight, and zero reward trajectories get zero weight.
- [4:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=265s) Key fact: for an action drawn from the policy, the expected gradient of its log probability is zero.
- [5:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=330s) Proof: write the expectation as an integral, the gradient of log p cancels with p, and the integral of the gradient of p is the gradient of the integral of p, which is the gradient of 1, which is zero.
- [7:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=426s) Intuitively, with no weights (no preference between actions) the gradient is zero, since no action is good or bad.
- [7:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=458s) Consequently you can multiply the score by any constant that does not depend on the action a_t without changing the expectation.
- [9:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=554s) Split the total reward into rewards before time t and rewards from time t on.
- [10:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=623s) Rewards before time t are constants from the point of view of a_t, since a_t has no effect on the past. Conditioning on the history, the expected score is zero, so those terms vanish (using the Markov property).
- [14:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=865s) You cannot change the past reward, so it should not weight your decision. Only the future reward matters.
- [15:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=900s) The remaining weight is the reward to go, the sum of future rewards, and the gradient is the score at each step weighted by its reward to go.
- [16:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=978s) Baseline: you may also subtract any term that depends only on the state s_t, and the expectation of the gradient is unchanged.
- [17:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=1045s) A baseline reduces the variance of the estimate. Example: if the reward is always between 10 and 11, subtracting 10.5 brings the weights to between minus 0.5 and 0.5.
- [19:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=1142s) It works like a control variate in statistics: subtract something correlated whose mean is zero.
- [19:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=1177s) A constant alone does not help much. The baseline must depend on the state, since rewards in different states can sit on different scales.
- [22:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=1369s) Without a baseline, when rewards are all positive you reinforce every action and only the better ones more. With a baseline, bad actions are explicitly penalized.
- [23:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=1433s) PPO remains somewhat of a mystery: why and when it works, especially in theory. None of the existing justifications is truly convincing, and training stability is a big practical problem.
- [26:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=1595s) Every policy gradient algorithm: sample trajectories from the current policy, estimate the gradient by the empirical mean over them (for example 128 trajectories), and update theta by a learning rate times that estimate.
- [27:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=1664s) The algorithm is on policy: the gradient estimator needs trajectories sampled from the current theta, so you cannot take two steps on the same samples.
- [29:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=1758s) PPO reuses samples from an old policy with importance sampling, correcting for the difference between the old and current policy.
- [30:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=1822s) Importance sampling fixes the action distribution only. The state distribution still comes from the old policy, which is an approximation.
- [34:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=2086s) Importance sampling identity: an expectation under p equals an expectation under q of p over q times the function.
- [36:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=2185s) People subtract a baseline and use an advantage, the reward of an action minus the baseline. Positive advantage means a good action and negative means a bad one.
- [38:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=2321s) The probability ratio r_t, new policy probability over old policy probability, can become too large or too small, which makes updates unstable.
- [40:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=2419s) PPO clips: with positive advantage and ratio above 1 plus epsilon, the action is already more likely than under the old policy, so the gradient is zeroed. Epsilon is a hyperparameter such as 0.2.
- [41:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=2484s) With positive advantage and a small ratio, the policy is worse than the old one at a good action, so the normal gradient is applied.
- [43:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=2586s) With negative advantage and ratio below 1 minus epsilon, the bad action is already less likely, so the gradient is zeroed. If the ratio is above that, the normal gradient is applied to push the probability down.
- [44:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=2652s) The published formula is the minimum of r_t times A and clip(r_t) times A. It encodes exactly the four cases above, where a clipped branch is a constant with no gradient.
- [48:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=2918s) The initial motivation was to stay near the old policy so the approximation still makes sense. The cost is slower learning.
- [49:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=2981s) Some people use a higher upper threshold than lower one, since less aggressive clipping on the high side seems less of a problem.
- [50:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3045s) Punishing bad actions too hard can lose diversity, making the policy too deterministic. Giving a bad action some chance may let it help in combination with others.
- [51:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3109s) A newer variant popular in the lecturer's lab keeps some gradient where PPO zeroes it. The captions garble its name, so it is not verified here.
- [55:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3301s) Clipping also avoids huge ratios. Since the ratio compares two probabilities, it can be large (a probability moving from 0.2 to 0.6 gives a ratio of 3), and a few such terms could dominate an update. Every few months there is a new way to clip, and it remains open research.
- [56:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3397s) From questions to answers: a language model can answer immediately, but letting it think first often works better, using reasoning tokens before the answer.
- [57:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3460s) Reasoning tokens may be delimited by special tokens, though some models do not use them.
- [58:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3524s) Few shot chain of thought: put worked examples with step by step solutions in the prompt, then pose the new question.
- [59:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3587s) Zero shot chain of thought: just add let's think step by step. This can raise accuracy by 5 to 20 percent or more.
- [60:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3652s) Teaching reasoning by supervised fine tuning on human written thoughts is limited, because it is not clear which way of thinking is best.
- [61:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3683s) Real reasoning traces of open models are messy, full of hesitation and backtracking. Studies of concise human style reasoning found it less useful for the model.
- [62:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3747s) RL needs no ready made answers, only a way to check whether the final answer is correct.
- [63:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3814s) Reward only the final response and ignore the thinking. Any thought process is fine as long as the answer is right.
- [64:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3877s) Language model as an MDP: the state is the prompt plus all tokens generated so far, and the action is the next token.
- [65:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3941s) The transition is deterministic: the next state is the old state with the new token appended. It is still hard to control since a long sequence of tokens must be right.
- [66:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=3972s) The reward is given only at the end of the trajectory.
- [66:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=4003s) The reward checks the answer against a reference answer, which requires a dataset of problems with known answers, such as math problems.
- [67:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=4034s) The reward is often binary: 1 if the answer matches, 0 otherwise. Formatting must make the answer extractable, for example inside answer tags parsed by a program.
- [68:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=4137s) PPO or a GRPO style algorithm can then be applied, with the ratio taken per token between the new and old policy probabilities of the token given the prompt and previous tokens.
- [70:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=4240s) Baseline for language models: sample about eight responses to the same question and average their rewards, instead of learning a value function.
- [71:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=4306s) If one response of eight is correct, its advantage is 7 over 8 and the others get minus 1 over 8. With four of eight correct the advantages are plus or minus 0.5. One can also divide by the standard deviation.
- [73:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=4402s) The policy must start with a nonzero chance of success. Models with almost no reasoning ability give zero reward and learn nothing.
- [74:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=4499s) SFT can create data with thinking and reflection to get started, and pretraining data may already contain extended thought processes.
- [76:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=4562s) A language model can serve as the reward function when there is no reference answer, but it can be gamed by responses that exploit style or format. How hard a giant model judge is to break is open.
- [77:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7CossjMvEg&t=4630s) Each iteration has three phases: generate trajectories, compute rewards, take gradient steps. Generation is slower than training because it is sequential and needs the KV cache.

### Formulas

The policy gradient (REINFORCE) from the last lecture, as a reward weighted sum of score functions:

$$
\nabla_\theta\eta=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big)\Big(\sum_{t}\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big)\Big]
$$

The expected score is zero (so anything not depending on the action can multiply it without changing the expectation):

$$
\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big]=\int\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,da=\nabla_\theta\!\int\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,da=\nabla_\theta 1=0
$$

Reward to go, and the baseline version with an advantage A hat (b depends on the state only):

$$
\nabla_\theta\eta=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\hat R_{\ge t}\Big],\qquad
\hat R_{\ge t}=\sum_{t'\ge t}\gamma^{t'}R(s_{t'}),\qquad \hat A_t=\hat R_{\ge t}-b(s_t)
$$

Importance sampling, and the probability ratio between the current and old policy:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\big[f(x)\big]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\Big[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}f(x)\Big],\qquad
r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}
$$

The PPO clipped objective to maximize (the clipped branch has no gradient):

$$
J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\min\Big(r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}\big(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\hat A_t\Big)\Big]
$$

For a language model the action is a token, the state is the prompt and the tokens so far, and the ratio is per token. Group baseline over G sampled responses to the same prompt:

$$
r_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},\qquad
\hat A^{(i)}=R^{(i)}-\frac1G\sum_{j=1}^{G}R^{(j)}
$$
